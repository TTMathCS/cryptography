# 15. Elliptic Curves

*Diffie-Hellman, DSA and Schnorr need numbers of 2048 bits or more, because the
integers mod $p$ have structure that the number field sieve exploits. This
chapter builds a different group, the points on an elliptic curve, where no such
shortcut is known, so 256-bit keys are enough. You will add points on a curve by
drawing lines, then do the same arithmetic mod a prime by hand, and see how
ECDH, ECDSA and EdDSA reuse everything from the last three chapters. Then the
practical part: which curve to pick, why X25519 and Ed25519 became the defaults,
and how a curve-based random generator became the best-known backdoor in
cryptography.*

## 15.1 The problem: keys that are too big

By the early 1980s the price of Diffie-Hellman and DSA was clear. The best
attack on discrete logs mod $p$, a sieve that uses the fact that integers factor
into primes, is far faster than the generic $\sqrt N$ attacks of
[Chapter 12](12-Diffie-Hellman.md). To stay ahead of it, $p$ must have thousands
of bits, and every key exchange, signature and certificate carries those big
numbers around. For a smart card of the time, a 1024-bit exponentiation was a
serious job.

What was needed was a group in which:

1. the group operation is cheap to compute,
2. elements can be written down compactly, and
3. there is no structure like "factoring into small primes" for a sieve to grab,
   so that the generic $\sqrt N$ attack is the best there is.

In 1985 Neal Koblitz at the University of Washington and Victor Miller at IBM
proposed, independently, the group of points on an **elliptic curve** over a
finite field. Hendrik Lenstra had just shown, that same year, that elliptic
curves could factor numbers (his elliptic curve method), which had brought the
curves to cryptographers' attention.

The curves themselves are old. Diophantus of Alexandria, around AD 250, found
rational points on curves of this shape by a trick that amounts to drawing a
line through known points, and in 1621 Claude-Gaspard Bachet found the formula
that doubles a rational point on $y^2 = x^3 + c$ by its tangent. Arab mathematicians of the 10th century asked which
whole numbers are the area of a right triangle with rational sides, the
"congruent number problem", which turns out to be a question about the curves
$y^2 = x^3 - n^2 x$. Henri Poincaré in 1901 studied the whole set of rational
points under the chord-and-tangent operation; Louis Mordell proved in 1922 that the rational points of such a
curve are generated by finitely many of them; and in 1995 Andrew Wiles's proof of
Fermat's Last Theorem went through elliptic curves. Cryptographers borrowed a
very well understood object.

Koblitz, a number theorist who had worked on these curves for years, and Miller,
a researcher at IBM's Thomas J. Watson lab, saw the same thing within months of
each other: the curves gave a group with all the properties Diffie-Hellman needed,
and none of the structure the sieves fed on. Many cryptographers at first
distrusted an idea that rested on such abstract mathematics, and it took about
twenty years for the distrust to turn into the default.

!!! note "Life story: elliptic-curve cryptography"
    **Born:** 1985, Neal Koblitz (University of Washington) and Victor Miller (IBM), independently · **Purpose:**
    a discrete-log group with no sieve attack, so keys can be small · **Helped:** smart cards, mobile devices,
    Bitcoin, and today almost every TLS, SSH and messaging handshake · **Status:** current; deprecated by NIST after
    2030 and disallowed after 2035 because Shor's algorithm breaks it · **Replaced by:** ML-KEM and ML-DSA, through
    hybrids with X25519

Adoption was slow. The mathematics was unfamiliar, there were patent worries
(the Canadian company Certicom held many), and RSA was entrenched. NSA's "Suite
B" (2005) endorsed curves, Daniel J. Bernstein's Curve25519 (2006) and Ed25519
(2011) made them fast and hard to misuse, and today an elliptic-curve key
exchange protects nearly every HTTPS connection.

## 15.2 Curves over the real numbers

An **elliptic curve** (for our purposes) is the set of points $(x, y)$ satisfying

$$
y^2 = x^3 + a x + b ,
$$

the **short Weierstrass form**, with the condition $4a^3 + 27b^2 \ne 0$, which
rules out curves with a cusp or a self-crossing. (The name comes from elliptic
integrals, which measure arc length on ellipses; the curves are not ellipses.)
The curve is symmetric about the $x$-axis, since $(x, -y)$ is on it whenever
$(x, y)$ is.

Take $y^2 = x^3 + 17$. Some of its points with whole-number coordinates are
$(-2, 3)$, $(-1, 4)$, $(2, 5)$ and $(4, 9)$ (and their mirror images).

### The chord-and-tangent rule

Here is the idea that makes points into a group. A straight line meets a cubic
curve in at most three points. So:

- **To add $P$ and $Q$:** draw the line through them. It meets the curve at one
  more point $R$. Reflect $R$ in the $x$-axis. That is $P + Q$.
- **To double $P$:** use the tangent line at $P$ instead.
- **The zero:** add one extra point $O$, the **point at infinity**, thought of as
  sitting infinitely far up (and down) every vertical line. A vertical line
  through $P = (x, y)$ meets the curve again at $(x, -y)$ and then at $O$, so
  $P + (x, -y) = O$. The inverse of $P$ is its mirror image: $-P = (x, -y)$.

### The formulas

Turning the picture into algebra: for $P = (x_1, y_1)$ and $Q = (x_2, y_2)$, the
line has slope

$$
m = \frac{y_2 - y_1}{x_2 - x_1} \quad (P \ne Q), \qquad m = \frac{3x_1^2 + a}{2y_1} \quad (P = Q,\ \text{the tangent}),
$$

and substituting the line into the curve and comparing coefficients of $x^2$ gives
the third intersection. After the reflection:

$$
x_3 = m^2 - x_1 - x_2, \qquad y_3 = m(x_1 - x_3) - y_1 .
$$

**By hand,** the picture's sum: $P = (-1, 4)$ plus $Q = (4, 9)$ on $y^2 = x^3 + 17$
($a = 0$):

$$
m = \frac{9 - 4}{4 - (-1)} = 1, \qquad x_3 = 1^2 - (-1) - 4 = -2, \qquad
y_3 = 1 \cdot (-1 - (-2)) - 4 = -3 .
$$

So $P + Q = (-2, -3)$. Check: $(-2)^3 + 17 = 9 = (-3)^2$. Whole-number answers
are luck. Adding $(-1, 4)$ and $(2, 5)$ instead gives slope $\tfrac13$,
$x_3 = \tfrac19 + 1 - 2 = -\tfrac89$ and $y_3 = \tfrac13(-1 + \tfrac89) - 4 = -\tfrac{109}{27}$;
check: $(-8/9)^3 + 17 = 11881/729 = (109/27)^2$. In Python, with exact fractions:

```python
from fractions import Fraction as F

def add_q(P, Q, a=0):                    # chord and tangent over the rationals
    (x1, y1), (x2, y2) = P, Q
    m = (y2 - y1) / (x2 - x1) if P != Q else (3 * x1 * x1 + a) / (2 * y1)
    x3 = m * m - x1 - x2
    return x3, m * (x1 - x3) - y1

P, Q = (F(-1), F(4)), (F(2), F(5))      # two points on y^2 = x^3 + 17
print(*add_q(P, Q))                      # -8/9 -109/27
Q2 = add_q(Q, Q)
print(*Q2)                               # -64/25 59/125
print(*add_q(Q2, Q))                     # 5023/3249 -842480/185193
```

Notice how fast the fractions grow: $Q$, $2Q$, $3Q$ have denominators 1, 125
and 185,193. Over the rationals, $kQ$ needs about $k^2$ times as many digits as
$Q$, which makes the rationals useless for cryptography. The fix is the same as
always: do the arithmetic mod a prime.

**The group laws.** Closure, identity and inverses are clear from the picture,
and $P + Q = Q + P$ because the line through $P$ and $Q$ is the line through $Q$
and $P$. Associativity, $(P + Q) + R = P + (Q + R)$, is true but not obvious; it
can be checked from the formulas by a long computation, or proved from deeper
geometry (it follows from the Cayley-Bacharach theorem on cubics). So the points
form an abelian group, and everything from [Chapter 12](12-Diffie-Hellman.md)
applies.

## 15.3 Curves over a finite field

Take the same formulas and do every operation mod a prime $p$, with division
meaning multiplication by an inverse. The "curve" is now a finite set of points,
scattered with no visible shape, but the group laws still hold, because they are
algebraic identities.

Our running example: $y^2 = x^3 + 2x + 2$ over $\mathbb{F}_{17}$. Trying all
$17 \times 17$ pairs finds 18 points, and with $O$ the group has 19 elements.
Since 19 is prime, every point except $O$ generates the whole group (Lagrange).
Take $G = (5, 1)$.

**Is $G$ on the curve?** $1^2 = 1$ and $5^3 + 2 \cdot 5 + 2 = 137 = 8 \cdot 17 + 1$. Yes.

**$2G$, by the tangent rule.**

| Step | Calculation | Value |
|---|---|---|
| slope | $m = (3 \cdot 5^2 + 2) / (2 \cdot 1) = 77 \cdot 2^{-1}$; $77 \equiv 9$ and $2^{-1} \equiv 9$ (as $2 \cdot 9 = 18 \equiv 1$) | $m = 81 \bmod 17 = 13$ |
| $x_3$ | $13^2 - 5 - 5 = 159$ | $159 \bmod 17 = 6$ |
| $y_3$ | $13 \cdot (5 - 6) - 1 = -14$ | $-14 \bmod 17 = 3$ |

So $2G = (6, 3)$.

1. $1G = (5, 1)$. A private key is a number $d$; the public key is the point $dG$.
2. $2G = (6, 3)$, by the tangent at $G$. $3G = (10, 6)$, by the line through $G$ and $2G$.

5. $18G = (5, 16) = -G$, the mirror image of $G$.
6. $19G = O$, the point at infinity. Going back from a point to its $d$ is the hard part.

```python
def add(P, Q):
    # None is the point at infinity, the group's zero
    if P is None: return Q
    if Q is None: return P
    (x1, y1), (x2, y2) = P, Q
    if x1 == x2 and (y1 + y2) % p == 0: return None    # P + (-P)
    if P == Q: m = (3 * x1 * x1 + a) * pow(2 * y1, -1, p) % p
    else: m = (y2 - y1) * pow(x2 - x1, -1, p) % p
    x3 = (m * m - x1 - x2) % p
    return x3, (m * (x1 - x3) - y1) % p

def mul(k, P):
    # double-and-add, one bit of k at a time
    R = None
    while k:
        if k & 1: R = add(R, P)
        P = add(P, P)
        k >>= 1
    return R

p, a, b = 17, 2, 2                       # y^2 = x^3 + 2x + 2 over F_17
```

**How many points?** Helmut Hasse proved in 1933 that a curve over
$\mathbb{F}_p$ has $p + 1 - t$ points with $\lvert t \rvert \le 2\sqrt p$. For $p = 17$
that allows 10 to 26; ours has 19. For a 256-bit $p$, the count is also about
256 bits. Counting points exactly takes René Schoof's algorithm (1985) and its
successors; that is how designers find curves whose point count is a prime, or a
prime times a small **cofactor** such as 4 or 8.

The code blocks in §15.4 and §15.6 reuse these `add` and `mul` functions with
other curve constants.

## 15.4 The elliptic-curve discrete logarithm

*Level: intermediate.* Given $G$ and $Q = dG$, find $d$. This is the
**elliptic-curve discrete logarithm problem** (ECDLP). In our toy group of 19
points it is trivial. In a group of about $2^{256}$ points:

- **Generic attacks** work as in [Chapter 12](12-Diffie-Hellman.md):
  baby-step giant-step and Pollard's rho need about $\sqrt n$ steps for a group
  of prime order $n$. For $n \approx 2^{256}$ that is $2^{128}$, and Pollard's rho
  spreads across many machines but gains nothing else.
- **No sieve is known.** The number field sieve works mod $p$ because integers
  have a notion of "small" and factor into small primes. Points on a curve have
  no useful analogue: lifting them to curves over the rationals, as Victor Miller
  already argued in 1985, runs into the explosion of sizes shown in §15.2.
  For well-chosen curves over prime fields, nothing better than $\sqrt n$ is known,
  after forty years of trying.

So a curve of 256 bits gives about 128-bit security, where finite-field groups and
RSA need 3072 bits:

| Security (bits) | Symmetric | Elliptic curve | RSA or finite-field DH |
|---|---|---|---|
| 80 | (2TDEA) | 160 | 1024 |
| 112 | 3TDEA | 224 | 2048 |
| 128 | AES-128 | 256 | 3072 |
| 192 | AES-192 | 384 | 7680 |
| 256 | AES-256 | 512 | 15360 |

**Special curves can be weak.** Some families have structure that does help:

- **Anomalous curves**, with exactly $p$ points, fall in polynomial time (Semaev,
  Satoh and Araki, Smart, 1997 to 1999).
- **Curves with a small embedding degree**: a pairing maps the ECDLP into a
  finite field $\mathbb{F}_{p^k}$, where the sieve works (Menezes, Okamoto and
  Vanstone, 1993: the MOV attack). For random curves $k$ is huge; for
  supersingular curves it can be 2.
- **Group order with only small factors**: Pohlig-Hellman, exactly as in
  [Chapter 12](12-Diffie-Hellman.md).

Standards choose curves that avoid all three.

## 15.5 ECDH

*Level: intermediate.* Diffie-Hellman carries over word for word, with $g^a$
replaced by $aG$:

$$
\text{Alice sends } A = d_A G, \quad \text{Bob sends } B = d_B G, \quad \text{both compute } d_A B = d_B A = d_A d_B \, G .
$$

**By hand** on the toy curve with $G = (5, 1)$: Alice picks $d_A = 3$ and sends
$A = 3G = (10, 6)$; Bob picks $d_B = 7$ and sends $B = 7G = (0, 6)$. Alice
computes $3B$ and Bob $7A$; both get $21G = 2G = (6, 3)$, since $21 \bmod 19 = 2$.

### X25519

In practice the dominant ECDH is **X25519**, Bernstein's function on
**Curve25519** (2006, specified in RFC 7748 in 2016). The curve is

$$
y^2 = x^3 + 486662 x^2 + x \quad \text{over } \mathbb{F}_p, \quad p = 2^{255} - 19,
$$

a **Montgomery curve** (Peter Montgomery, 1987). Its design choices are all about
making correct, constant-time code easy:

- **Only $x$ is used.** The Montgomery ladder computes the $x$-coordinate of $dP$
  from the $x$-coordinate of $P$ alone, doing the same steps for every bit of $d$.
  Public keys and shared secrets are 32 bytes.
- **Every 32-byte string is accepted as a public key.** A value that is not the
  $x$ of a point on the curve is the $x$ of a point on its **twist**, a related
  curve, and Curve25519 was chosen so that the twist is also secure. There is no
  validation step to forget.
- **Clamping.** The secret scalar has its three low bits cleared (a multiple of
  the cofactor 8, which kills small-subgroup components) and bit 254 set (so every
  key takes the same number of ladder steps).
- **Fast arithmetic.** $2^{255} - 19$ is close to a power of 2, so reduction is
  cheap.

```python
def ladder(k, u, p, a24, bits):
    # Montgomery ladder, RFC 7748 section 5: works on u (= x) alone
    x2, z2, x3, z3, swap = 1, 0, u, 1, 0
    for t in reversed(range(bits)):
        b = k >> t & 1
        if swap ^ b: x2, x3, z2, z3 = x3, x2, z3, z2
        swap = b
        A, B, C, D = x2 + z2, x2 - z2, x3 + z3, x3 - z3
        AA, BB, DA, CB = A * A, B * B, D * A, C * B
        E = AA - BB
        x3, z3 = (DA + CB) ** 2 % p, u * (DA - CB) ** 2 % p
        x2, z2 = AA * BB % p, E * (AA + a24 * E) % p
    if swap: x2, z2 = x3, z3
    return x2 * pow(z2, p - 2, p) % p

def x25519(k, u):
    k = int.from_bytes(k, "little") & (1 << 254) - 8 | 1 << 254    # clamp
    u = int.from_bytes(u, "little") & (1 << 255) - 1
    return ladder(k, u, 2**255 - 19, 121665, 255).to_bytes(32, "little")

h = bytes.fromhex                                      # RFC 7748 section 6.1
a = h("77076d0a7318a57d3c16c17251b26645df4c2f87ebc0992ab177fba51db92c2a")
b = h("5dab087e624a8a4b79e17f8b83800ee66f3bb1292618b6fd1c2f8b27ff88e0eb")
A, B = x25519(a, b"\x09" + bytes(31)), x25519(b, b"\x09" + bytes(31))
print(A.hex()[:16], B.hex()[:16])                      # 8520f0098930a754 de9edb7d7b7dc1b4
print(x25519(a, B) == x25519(b, A), x25519(a, B).hex()[:16])   # True 4a5d9d5ba4ce2de1
print(x25519(a, bytes(32)) == bytes(32))               # True: a low-order point gives all zeros
```

The outputs match the test vectors of RFC 7748. The last line is a warning: a
peer who sends one of the few points of small order forces the result to all
zeros whatever your key. That does not leak your key, but a protocol that needs
both sides to contribute should reject an all-zero result, as TLS 1.3 does. And
as with all Diffie-Hellman, the raw result goes through a KDF before use.

### The invalid-curve attack on Weierstrass curves

ECDH on the NIST curves needs a check X25519 does not: that a received point
really is on the curve. Look at the addition formulas in §15.2: they use $a$ but
never $b$. So if an attacker sends a point $(x, y)$ that is not on P-256, the
victim's code happily computes on the curve $y^2 = x^3 + ax + b'$ that the point
*is* on, and the attacker can choose $b'$ so that this curve has a subgroup of
small order. The result leaks the victim's key mod that small order, exactly as
in the small-subgroup attack of [Chapter 12](12-Diffie-Hellman.md); a few dozen
such points and the Chinese remainder theorem give the whole key (Biehl, Meyer
and Müller, 2000). In 2015 Tibor Jager, Jörg Schwenk and Juraj Somorovsky pulled
TLS server keys out of two popular Java libraries this way, and in 2018 Eli Biham
and Lior Neumann found that Bluetooth pairing checked only $x$-coordinates
(CVE-2018-5383). **Validate every point:** on the curve, not $O$, coordinates
in range.

!!! note "Life story: the invalid-curve attack"
    **Born:** 2000, Ingrid Biehl, Bernd Meyer and Volker Müller · **Purpose:** an attack on ECDH code that does
    not check received points lie on the curve · **Impact:** pulled TLS keys from Java libraries (2015) and broke
    Bluetooth pairing (2018); made point validation mandatory · **Status:** defeated by validation, or by
    x-only designs like X25519

## 15.6 ECDSA

*Level: intermediate.* Scott Vanstone of the University of Waterloo, a co-founder
of Certicom, proposed ECDSA in 1992 in response to NIST's call for comments on
DSA. It became ANSI X9.62 (1999) and part of FIPS 186-2 (2000). It is DSA from
[Chapter 14](14-Discrete-Log-Systems.md) with $g^k \bmod p$ replaced by the
$x$-coordinate of $kG$:

$$
\begin{aligned}
\textbf{Keys:} \quad & d \in [1, n-1], \quad Q = dG \\
\textbf{Sign:} \quad & \text{fresh } k, \quad r = (kG)_x \bmod n, \quad s = k^{-1}(H(m) + r d) \bmod n \\
\textbf{Verify:} \quad & w = s^{-1}, \quad X = (H(m) w) \, G + (r w) \, Q, \quad \text{accept if } X_x \bmod n = r
\end{aligned}
$$

**Why it works:** $(H w) G + (r w) d G = s^{-1}(H + r d) G = k G$.

**By hand** on the toy curve ($G = (5, 1)$, $n = 19$), with private key $d = 7$:

| Step | Calculation | Value |
|---|---|---|
| Public key | $Q = 7G$ | $(0, 6)$ |
| Hash (made up) | $H(m)$ | 10 |
| Nonce | $k = 3$, $kG = (10, 6)$ | |
| $r$ | $x$-coordinate of $kG$, mod 19 | $r = 10$ |
| $s$ | $3^{-1} \cdot (10 + 10 \cdot 7) \bmod 19 = 13 \cdot 80 \bmod 19$ | $s = 14$ |
| Verify: $w$ | $14^{-1} \bmod 19$ | $w = 15$ |
| $u_1 = H w$, $u_2 = r w$ | $150 \bmod 19$, $150 \bmod 19$ | $u_1 = u_2 = 17$ |
| $X = 17G + 17Q$ | $(6, 14) + (9, 16)$ | $(10, 6)$ |
| Check | $10 \bmod 19 = r$ | valid |

At full size on secp256k1, Bitcoin's curve ($y^2 = x^3 + 7$), reusing `add` and
`mul` from §15.3:

```python
import hashlib, secrets

# secp256k1 (SEC 2): y^2 = x^3 + 7 over F_p, base point G of prime order n
p, a = 2**256 - 2**32 - 977, 0
n = 0xFFFFFFFFFFFFFFFFFFFFFFFFFFFFFFFEBAAEDCE6AF48A03BBFD25E8CD0364141
G = (0x79BE667EF9DCBBAC55A06295CE870B07029BFCDB2DCE28D959F2815B16F81798,
     0x483ADA7726A3C4655DA4FBFC0E1108A8FD17B448A68554199C47D08FFB10D4B8)

def Hn(msg):
    return int.from_bytes(hashlib.sha256(msg).digest(), "big") % n

def sign(msg, d, k=None):
    k = k or secrets.randbelow(n - 1) + 1
    r = mul(k, G)[0] % n
    s = pow(k, -1, n) * (Hn(msg) + r * d) % n
    return r, s

def verify(msg, Q, r, s):
    if not (0 < r < n and 0 < s < n): return False
    w = pow(s, -1, n)
    X = add(mul(Hn(msg) * w % n, G), mul(r * w % n, Q))
    return X is not None and X[0] % n == r

d = secrets.randbelow(n - 1) + 1         # private key: a number
Q = mul(d, G)                            # public key: a point
r, s = sign(b"pay 1 BTC to Bob", d)
print(verify(b"pay 1 BTC to Bob", Q, r, s), verify(b"pay 9 BTC to Bob", Q, r, s))
print(verify(b"pay 1 BTC to Bob", Q, r, n - s))   # True: (r, n - s) is valid too

k = secrets.randbelow(n - 1) + 1         # the PS3 mistake: the same k twice
r1, s1 = sign(b"game one", d, k)
r2, s2 = sign(b"game two", d, k)
k_found = (Hn(b"game one") - Hn(b"game two")) * pow(s1 - s2, -1, n) % n
print((s1 * k_found - Hn(b"game one")) * pow(r1, -1, n) % n == d)   # True
```

Output:

```
True False
True
True
```

These functions interoperate with PyCA `cryptography`'s ECDSA on `SECP256K1`
(signatures decoded to $(r, s)$). They are not constant time; use them to learn.

Three things the output shows:

- **Malleability.** $-kG$ has the same $x$-coordinate as $kG$, so $(r, n - s)$ is
  a second valid signature on the same message. Bitcoin learned this the hard way
  (transactions could be altered in flight, changing their IDs), and its relay
  rules now require the "low-$s$" form.
- **The nonce, again.** Everything in [§14.5](14-Discrete-Log-Systems.md) applies
  unchanged: Sony's PS3 key (2010), the Android Bitcoin wallets (2013), and the
  timing attacks Minerva and TPM-Fail (2019) were all ECDSA.
- **Verification bugs.** In 2020 Windows accepted certificates whose ECDSA key
  came with attacker-chosen curve parameters, letting an attacker supply their own
  generator (CurveBall, CVE-2020-0601, reported by the NSA). In 2022 Java 15 to 18
  accepted the signature $r = s = 0$ for any message and any key ("psychic
  signatures", CVE-2022-21449); the range check in `verify` above is the line that
  was missing.

Vanstone, a mathematician at Waterloo, had co-founded Certicom in 1985 to turn
curves into products, and for years the company was elliptic-curve cryptography's
main advocate and patent holder. In 2003 the NSA paid Certicom for a license to
some of its patents, which helped clear the way for Suite B.

!!! note "Life story: ECDSA"
    **Born:** 1992, Scott Vanstone (University of Waterloo, Certicom), as a comment on NIST's DSA proposal;
    ANSI X9.62 (1999) and FIPS 186-2 (2000) · **Purpose:** DSA's signature on an elliptic curve, far smaller ·
    **Helped:** most TLS certificates issued today, Bitcoin and Ethereum transactions, passkeys, smart cards ·
    **Setbacks:** nonce disasters (PS3 2010, Android 2013, Minerva 2019), malleability, CurveBall and psychic
    signatures · **Status:** current, with RFC 6979 nonces; EdDSA preferred for new designs

## 15.7 EdDSA and Ed25519

*Level: intermediate.* Ed25519 (Daniel J. Bernstein, Niels Duif, Tanja Lange, Peter
Schwabe and Bo-Yin Yang, 2011) is a Schnorr signature
([Chapter 14](14-Discrete-Log-Systems.md)) on a **twisted Edwards curve**:

$$
-x^2 + y^2 = 1 + d x^2 y^2 \quad \text{over } \mathbb{F}_p, \quad p = 2^{255} - 19, \quad d = -121665/121666 .
$$

It is the same group as Curve25519, written in different coordinates. Edwards
curves (Harold Edwards, 2007; Bernstein and Lange for cryptography) have one
addition formula that works for every pair of points, including doubling and the
neutral point $(0, 1)$, with no special cases:

$$
x_3 = \frac{x_1 y_2 + y_1 x_2}{1 + d x_1 x_2 y_1 y_2}, \qquad
y_3 = \frac{y_1 y_2 + x_1 x_2}{1 - d x_1 x_2 y_1 y_2} .
$$

No branches means no timing leaks from branches, and no rare inputs that give
wrong answers.

**The scheme** (RFC 8032, 2017):

- A 32-byte seed is hashed with SHA-512. The first half, clamped, is the secret
  scalar $s$; the public key is $A = sB$. The second half is a secret prefix.
- **Sign** $M$: $r = H(\mathit{prefix} \,\Vert\, M) \bmod \ell$, $R = rB$,
  $S = (r + H(R \,\Vert\, A \,\Vert\, M) \cdot s) \bmod \ell$. The signature is $(R, S)$,
  64 bytes.
- **Verify:** $S B = R + H(R \,\Vert\, A \,\Vert\, M) \cdot A$, and $S < \ell$.

The nonce $r$ is a hash of a secret and the message. It never repeats for
different messages, and there is no random generator to fail: the PS3 mistake is
impossible by construction. Hashing $A$ into the challenge also means one
signature can't be reused under a related key.

```python
from hashlib import sha512

p = 2**255 - 19
L = 2**252 + 27742317777372353535851937790883648493    # the prime group order
d = -121665 * pow(121666, -1, p) % p                   # the curve: -x^2 + y^2 = 1 + d x^2 y^2

def add(P, Q):                    # one formula for every case, extended coordinates (X, Y, Z, T)
    A = (P[1] - P[0]) * (Q[1] - Q[0]) % p
    B = (P[1] + P[0]) * (Q[1] + Q[0]) % p
    C, D = 2 * P[3] * Q[3] * d % p, 2 * P[2] * Q[2] % p
    E, F, G, H = B - A, D - C, D + C, B + A
    return E * F, G * H, F * G, E * H

def mul(s, P):
    Q = (0, 1, 1, 0)                                   # the neutral point (0, 1)
    while s:
        if s & 1: Q = add(Q, P)
        P, s = add(P, P), s >> 1
    return Q

def enc(P):                       # 32 bytes: y, with the low bit of x on top
    zi = pow(P[2], p - 2, p)
    x, y = P[0] * zi % p, P[1] * zi % p
    return (y | (x & 1) << 255).to_bytes(32, "little")

def dec(b):                       # None if b is not a canonical point
    y = int.from_bytes(b, "little")
    sign, y = y >> 255, y & (1 << 255) - 1
    if y >= p: return None
    x2 = (y * y - 1) * pow(d * y * y + 1, -1, p) % p
    x = pow(x2, (p + 3) // 8, p)
    if (x * x - x2) % p: x = x * pow(2, (p - 1) // 4, p) % p
    if (x * x - x2) % p or (x == 0 and sign): return None
    if x & 1 != sign: x = p - x
    return x, y, 1, x * y % p

B = dec((4 * pow(5, -1, p) % p).to_bytes(32, "little"))   # base point: y = 4/5
Hq = lambda b: int.from_bytes(sha512(b).digest(), "little") % L

def sign(seed, m):
    h = sha512(seed).digest()
    s = int.from_bytes(h[:32], "little") & (1 << 254) - 8 | 1 << 254   # clamped secret scalar
    A = enc(mul(s, B))
    r = Hq(h[32:] + m)                                 # the nonce comes from the message
    R = enc(mul(r, B))
    return A, R + ((r + Hq(R + A + m) * s) % L).to_bytes(32, "little")

def verify(A, m, sig):
    Ap, R, S = dec(A), dec(sig[:32]), int.from_bytes(sig[32:], "little")
    if Ap is None or R is None or S >= L: return False
    P, Q = mul(S, B), add(R, mul(Hq(sig[:32] + A + m), Ap))
    return (P[0] * Q[2] - Q[0] * P[2]) % p == 0 and (P[1] * Q[2] - Q[1] * P[2]) % p == 0

seed = bytes.fromhex("9d61b19deffd5a60ba844af492ec2cc44449c5697b326919703bac031cae7f60")
A, sig = sign(seed, b"")                               # RFC 8032 section 7.1, TEST 1
print(A.hex()[:16], sig.hex()[:16], verify(A, b"", sig))   # d75a980182b10ab7 e5564300c360ac72 True
print(sign(seed, b"")[1] == sig, verify(A, b"x", sig))     # True False: same signature every time
```

The public key and the full 64-byte signature match TEST 1 of RFC 8032.

**Pitfalls that remain.** A signing function must use the public key derived from
the secret, never one passed in by the caller: in 2022 dozens of libraries were
found whose API took the public key as a separate argument, and signing the same
message under a wrong public key gives two signatures with the same $r$ and
different challenges, which reveals the secret. Deterministic nonces also make
**fault attacks** easier: a glitch during one of two identical signings gives the
same situation. Hardware exposed to faults should add randomness to the nonce.
And libraries disagree on edge cases (non-canonical encodings, small-order
points, the cofactor), which matters when many machines must agree on validity,
as in a blockchain; ZIP 215 (2020) is one written-down answer. Details: [EdDSA
reference](../02-signatures/EdDSA.md).

!!! note "Life story: Curve25519, X25519 and Ed25519"
    **Born:** 2006 (Curve25519, Daniel J. Bernstein) and 2011 (Ed25519, Bernstein, Duif, Lange, Schwabe and Yang)
    · **Purpose:** curves and formulas on which the straightforward implementation is also the safe and fast one ·
    **Helped:** X25519 became TLS 1.3's most used key exchange and is in SSH, Signal, WireGuard; Ed25519 is the
    default SSH key and in FIPS 186-5 · **Status:** current; paired with ML-KEM in hybrids

### Use a library

```python
# pip install cryptography
from cryptography.hazmat.primitives.asymmetric import ec, x25519, ed25519
from cryptography.hazmat.primitives.kdf.hkdf import HKDF
from cryptography.hazmat.primitives import hashes

alice, bob = x25519.X25519PrivateKey.generate(), x25519.X25519PrivateKey.generate()
shared = alice.exchange(bob.public_key())                 # 32 bytes
assert shared == bob.exchange(alice.public_key())
key = HKDF(algorithm=hashes.SHA256(), length=32, salt=None, info=b"demo v1").derive(shared)

signer = ed25519.Ed25519PrivateKey.generate()
sig = signer.sign(b"release 1.2")                         # 64 bytes, deterministic
signer.public_key().verify(sig, b"release 1.2")           # raises InvalidSignature if bad

p256 = ec.generate_private_key(ec.SECP256R1())
sig2 = p256.sign(b"release 1.2", ec.ECDSA(hashes.SHA256()))
p256.public_key().verify(sig2, b"release 1.2", ec.ECDSA(hashes.SHA256()))
print(len(shared), len(key), len(sig), len(sig2) <= 72)   # 32 32 64 True
```

An ECDSA signature is DER-encoded, usually 70 to 72 bytes for P-256; Ed25519's is always 64.

## 15.8 Choosing a curve

*Level: intermediate.* Unlike AES, where everyone uses the same cipher, curves
come in competing families, and the choice has been political as well as
technical.

| Curve | Field prime | Shape | Cofactor | Origin | Main uses |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| P-256 (secp256r1) | $2^{256} - 2^{224} + 2^{192} + 2^{96} - 1$ | Weierstrass, $a = -3$ | 1 | NSA, published by NIST 1999, FIPS 186-2 (2000) | TLS certificates, smart cards, FIPS modules, WebAuthn |
| P-384, P-521 | special primes; $2^{521} - 1$ for P-521 | Weierstrass | 1 | same | government "top secret" profiles (P-384) |
| Curve25519 / Ed25519 | $2^{255} - 19$ | Montgomery / twisted Edwards | 8 | Bernstein, 2006; RFC 7748, RFC 8032 | TLS 1.3, SSH, Signal, WireGuard, age |
| Curve448 / Ed448 | $2^{448} - 2^{224} - 1$ | Montgomery / Edwards | 4 | Hamburg, 2015 | a higher-security option (about 224 bits) |
| secp256k1 | $2^{256} - 2^{32} - 977$ | Weierstrass, $y^2 = x^3 + 7$ | 1 | Certicom, SEC 2 (2000) | Bitcoin, Ethereum |
| Brainpool | from the digits of $\pi$ | Weierstrass | 1 | BSI and secunet, RFC 5639 (2010) | European government profiles |

**P-256 and the seeds.** Each NIST curve's constant $b$ was made by hashing a
160-bit seed with SHA-1. Anyone can check the hashing, but the seeds came with no
explanation, so whoever chose them could in principle have tried seed after seed
until a curve had a property only they knew how to exploit. No such weakness has
been found, and the families of weak curves known (§15.4) are rare enough that
finding one by searching seeds is not thought feasible. Still, after 2013 (below)
many people wanted curves whose every constant has an explanation. A 2026 report
traced the seeds to the output of the C library's `lrand48` generator started from
timestamps in 1997; the [Elliptic curves reference](../10-math-foundations/Elliptic-Curves.md)
reproduces P-256's seed that way.

**Curve25519 and rigidity.** Bernstein chose the prime $2^{255} - 19$ for speed and
then took the smallest coefficient $A = 486662$ that gives prime-order subgroups
with small cofactors on both the curve and its twist. Nothing is left for a
designer to tune. With Tanja Lange he built **SafeCurves**, a list of criteria
(twist security, complete formulas, constant-time ladders, explained constants);
Curve25519 and Ed448-Goldilocks pass, and the NIST curves, secp256k1 and Brainpool
each fail at least one. That doesn't make those curves broken; it makes safe code
for them harder to write.

!!! note "Life story: the NIST curves (P-256 and friends)"
    **Born:** 1999, generated at the NSA and published by NIST, in FIPS 186-2 (2000) · **Purpose:** standard curves
    for government and industry at several security levels · **Helped:** smart cards, TLS certificates, FIPS
    modules, WebAuthn passkeys · **Doubts:** unexplained seeds and formulas that are hard to implement safely ·
    **Status:** current, required by many standards

**secp256k1** has $a = 0$, which gives it an extra fast endomorphism (the GLV
method speeds up scalar multiplication). Satoshi Nakamoto chose it for Bitcoin in
2009; nobody knows exactly why, though its simple constants and the absence of
NIST's seeds are often suggested.

!!! note "Life story: secp256k1"
    **Born:** 2000, Certicom's SEC 2 · **Purpose:** a fast Koblitz-style curve with $a = 0$ · **Helped:** chosen by
    Satoshi Nakamoto for Bitcoin in 2009, then by Ethereum, whose keys it now protects · **Status:** current, with ECDSA and (since 2021) Schnorr signatures

**Cofactors.** Curve25519's group has $8\ell$ points for a prime $\ell$. Clamping
handles the cofactor in X25519, but protocols that need a prime-order group (for
example, to prove statements about group elements) have tripped over it. The
**ristretto255** and **decaf448** encodings (RFC 9496) give a clean prime-order
group on top of these curves.

**In short:** use X25519 for key exchange and Ed25519 for signatures unless a
standard requires P-256 (FIPS validation, WebAuthn hardware, many certificates),
and in that case use a library that validates points and computes in constant
time.

### Dual_EC_DRBG: a backdoor in one equation

The best-known suspected backdoor in modern cryptography was built from
elliptic-curve points. In 2006 NIST's SP 800-90 standardized four random-number
generators. One, Dual_EC_DRBG, promoted by the NSA, was far slower
than the others and used two fixed points $P$ and $Q$ on P-256 with no
explanation of where they came from. Its state update and output are:

$$
s_{i+1} = (s_i P)_x, \qquad \text{output } (s_{i+1} Q)_x \text{ with the top 16 bits dropped.}
$$

In 2007 Dan Shumow and Niels Ferguson of Microsoft pointed out the problem: if
whoever chose the points knows $e$ with $P = eQ$, then from one output they can
rebuild the point $s_{i+1} Q$ (trying the $2^{16}$ missing bits), multiply by $e$
to get $s_{i+1} P$, whose $x$-coordinate is the next state, and predict every later
output. Here is the idea on secp256k1, without the truncation, reusing the code of
§15.6:

```python
e = secrets.randbelow(n - 1) + 1         # the designer's secret
Qd = G                                   # the public point Q
Pd = mul(e, Qd)                          # the public point P = e*Q, which looks like any other point

state = secrets.randbelow(n)             # the generator's secret state
def dual_ec():                           # Dual_EC_DRBG without the truncation
    global state
    state = mul(state, Pd)[0]            # new state: x(s*P)
    return mul(state, Qd)[0]             # output: x(s*Q)

out1, out2 = dual_ec(), dual_ec()        # the victim's "random" numbers, e.g. a TLS nonce and a key

# The designer sees out1, rebuilds the point s*Q from its x, multiplies by e
y = pow((out1**3 + 7) % p, (p + 1) // 4, p)
s_next = mul(e, (out1, y))[0]            # e*(s*Q) = s*P, whose x is the next state
print(mul(s_next, Qd)[0] == out2)        # True: every later output is predictable
```

The documents Edward Snowden released in 2013 described an NSA program to insert
weaknesses into commercial encryption, and the *New York Times* reported that they
appeared to confirm the Dual_EC backdoor; the NSA has not acknowledged it. NIST
withdrew Dual_EC_DRBG in 2014. Reuters reported that RSA Security had made it the
default in its BSAFE library under a \$10 million contract, and in 2015 Juniper
Networks found that intruders had replaced the $Q$ in its firewalls' Dual_EC with
their own. The full story is in [the History section](../books/history/18-The-Internet-Era.md), and
[Chapter 4](04-Randomness-and-Perfect-Secrecy.md) covers random generators. The
lesson for curves: unexplained constants are a liability even when no attack is
known.

    **Born:** promoted by the NSA in the early 2000s; standardized in NIST SP 800-90 (2006) and ANSI X9.82 ·
    **Purpose:** officially, a random generator with a security argument from elliptic curves · **Warning:** Shumow
    and Ferguson showed in 2007 that its two points could hide a backdoor · **Retired:** NIST told users to stop
    using it in 2013 and withdrew it in 2014, after the Snowden documents · **Legacy:** the Juniper incident (2015)

## 15.9 In the real world

**Where you meet it.** X25519 is the most widely used key exchange on the
internet: TLS 1.3, SSH, WireGuard, Signal and age all use it, and it is
increasingly paired with ML-KEM in a hybrid. ECDSA with P-256 or P-384 signs a
growing share of TLS certificates, and most WebAuthn passkeys sign with ECDSA on
P-256.
Ed25519 has been OpenSSH's default key type since version 9.5 (2023) and signs
Git commits, DNSSEC zones (RFC 8080) and Tor relay identities. Bitcoin and
Ethereum use secp256k1, with ECDSA and, since Taproot (2021), Schnorr signatures.

**Standards.** RFC 7748 (X25519, X448), RFC 8032 (EdDSA), FIPS 186-5 (ECDSA and
EdDSA, 2023), SP 800-186 (the approved curves, 2023), SP 800-56A (ECDH), SEC 1 and
SEC 2 (Certicom's encodings and curves).

**Sizes and status.** 256-bit curves give about 128-bit security, 384-bit about
192, Curve448 about 224. All of them fall to Shor's algorithm
([Chapter 20](20-Quantum-Computers.md)), and estimates suggest a 256-bit curve
needs a smaller quantum computer than RSA-2048. NIST IR 8547 deprecates
elliptic-curve key exchange and signatures after 2030 and disallows them after
2035; the replacements are ML-KEM and ML-DSA ([Chapter 21](21-Lattice-Cryptography.md)).
Hybrids (X25519 with ML-KEM) are the bridge.

Full details: [Elliptic curves](../10-math-foundations/Elliptic-Curves.md),
[ECDH](../01-key-exchange/ECDH.md), [ECDSA](../02-signatures/ECDSA.md),
[EdDSA](../02-signatures/EdDSA.md), [Schnorr](../02-signatures/Schnorr.md).

### Projective coordinates

Every affine addition needs a field inversion, which costs as much as dozens of
multiplications. Real code works in **projective coordinates** $(X : Y : Z)$ with
$x = X/Z$, $y = Y/Z$, or Jacobian $(X : Y : Z)$ with $x = X/Z^2$, $y = Y/Z^3$, and
inverts once at the end. The Ed25519 code above uses **extended coordinates**
$(X, Y, Z, T)$ with $T = XY/Z$ (Hisil, Wong, Carter and Dawson, 2008), in which an
addition costs about 8 multiplications and no inversion.

### Why the Montgomery ladder works

The ladder keeps a pair $(R_0, R_1)$ with $R_1 - R_0 = P$ at every step: for each
bit, it replaces the pair by $(2R_0, R_0 + R_1)$ or $(R_0 + R_1, 2R_1)$. Because the
difference $P$ is always known, $x(R_0 + R_1)$ can be computed from $x(R_0)$,
$x(R_1)$ and $x(P)$ alone ("differential addition"). Every bit does one doubling
and one addition, in the same order, and the `swap` is done arithmetically in
constant-time code, so the sequence of operations does not depend on the key.
Peter Montgomery introduced it in 1987 for factoring with elliptic curves.

### Twist security

For a given $p$, every $x \in \mathbb{F}_p$ either is the $x$-coordinate of a point
on $E$ or of a point on its quadratic twist $E'$, and $\#E + \#E' = 2p + 2$. An
$x$-only ladder handed an $x$ from $E'$ computes on $E'$. If $\#E'$ has small
factors, an attacker who sends such $x$ values learns the key piece by piece, as
in the invalid-curve attack. Curve25519's twist has order $4 \cdot \ell'$ for a large
prime $\ell'$; SafeCurves puts the best attack via the twist at about $2^{124}$ for
Curve25519, about $2^{120}$ for P-256 and about $2^{110}$ for secp256k1, all safe,
but only $2^{58}$ for P-224 and $2^{44.5}$ for brainpoolP256t1. Code that validates
points never meets the twist.

### Pairings

A **pairing** is a map $e: G_1 \times G_2 \to G_T$ with $e(aP, bQ) = e(P, Q)^{ab}$.
On most curves it is useless (its target group is enormous). On specially built
"pairing-friendly" curves (Barreto-Naehrig, BLS12-381) it gives powerful tools:
**BLS signatures** (Boneh, Lynn and Shacham, 2001), which are a single point and can
be aggregated across many signers (Ethereum's consensus layer uses them),
identity-based encryption (Boneh and Franklin, 2001), and many zero-knowledge proof
systems ([Chapter 23](23-Advanced-Protocols.md)). The price is that the MOV
reduction now applies by design, so the field $\mathbb{F}_{p^k}$ must be large
enough to resist the sieve; improved sieves in 2016 pushed recommended sizes up.

### Isogenies

An **isogeny** is a map between two curves that respects the group law. Isogeny
problems were proposed as post-quantum foundations. SIDH and its KEM SIKE, a
fourth-round candidate in NIST's process, were broken in 2022 by Wouter Castryck
and Thomas Decru in about an hour on one core, using a 1997 theorem of Ernst Kani
and the extra points SIDH had to publish. Other isogeny schemes, such as CSIDH and
the signature SQIsign, are not affected. [Chapter 20](20-Quantum-Computers.md) and
[Hard problems](../10-math-foundations/Hard-Problems.md) say more.

!!! note "Life story: SIDH and SIKE"
    **Born:** 2011, David Jao and Luca De Feo (SIDH); SIKE submitted to NIST in 2017 · **Purpose:** post-quantum key
    exchange from isogenies, with tiny keys · **Broken:** July 2022 by Wouter Castryck and Thomas Decru, on a
    single core in about an hour · **Status:** dead; other isogeny schemes such as SQIsign survive

## Check yourself

??? question "1. Is $(3, 1)$ on $y^2 = x^3 + 2x + 2$ over $\mathbb{F}_{17}$? What is $-(3, 1)$?"

    $1^2 = 1$ and $27 + 6 + 2 = 35 = 2 \cdot 17 + 1$, so yes. Its negative is $(3, -1) = (3, 16)$,
    which is $15G$ in the list of §15.3 (and $(3, 1) = 4G$, so $4 + 15 = 19$, as it should be).

??? question "2. Compute $4G = 2G + 2G$ on the toy curve by hand, with $2G = (6, 3)$."

    Tangent slope $m = (3 \cdot 36 + 2)/(2 \cdot 3) = 110 \cdot 6^{-1}$. Mod 17, $110 \equiv 8$ and $6^{-1} \equiv 3$
    (since $18 \equiv 1$), so $m = 24 \equiv 7$. Then $x_3 = 49 - 12 = 37 \equiv 3$ and
    $y_3 = 7 \cdot (6 - 3) - 3 = 18 \equiv 1$. So $4G = (3, 1)$, as the table says.

??? question "3. Do ECDH on the toy curve with $d_A = 2$ and $d_B = 5$."

    Alice sends $2G = (6, 3)$, Bob sends $5G = (9, 16)$. Both compute $10G = (7, 11)$.

??? question "4. By Hasse's theorem, how many points can a curve over $\mathbb{F}_{17}$ have?"

    $p + 1 - t$ with $\lvert t \rvert \le 2\sqrt{17} \approx 8.2$, so between $18 - 8 = 10$ and $18 + 8 = 26$.

??? question "5. Why does a 256-bit elliptic-curve key give about the same security as a 3072-bit RSA key?"

    The best known attack on a good curve is generic (Pollard's rho), costing about $\sqrt n = 2^{128}$ steps for
    $n \approx 2^{256}$. RSA and finite-field groups are attacked by the number field sieve, which is
    subexponential, so their numbers must be far larger to reach $2^{128}$.

??? question "6. Given one ECDSA signature $(r, s)$ on a message, how do you make a second valid signature without the key? How do Ed25519 verifiers prevent the analogous trick of adding $\ell$ to $S$?"

    $(r, n - s)$ is valid, because $-kG$ and $kG$ have the same $x$-coordinate. Ed25519 verifiers must check
    $S < \ell$ (RFC 8032 §8.4); without that check $(R, S + \ell)$ would also verify.

??? question "7. Why doesn't X25519 need to check that a received public key is on the curve, while ECDH on P-256 does?"

    Every 32-byte value is the $x$-coordinate of a point either on Curve25519 or on its twist, the twist was
    chosen to be secure too, and the ladder never uses $y$. On P-256 the formulas ignore $b$, so an off-curve point
    lies on some other curve, possibly with a small subgroup, which leaks the key (the invalid-curve attack).

??? question "8. In Dual_EC_DRBG, why is knowing $e$ with $P = eQ$ enough to predict the generator?"

    An output is the $x$-coordinate of $sQ$, so it gives the point $\pm sQ$. Multiplying by $e$ gives $\pm sP$,
    and the next state is the $x$-coordinate of $sP$, the same for both signs. From the state, every later output
    follows. Without $e$, getting $sP$ from $sQ$ is a Diffie-Hellman problem.

??? question "9. ECDSA signatures from one key, made over a year, show the same $r$ twice. What happened, and what is the damage?"

    The same nonce $k$ was used twice (or the same $kG$, which for a random $k$ is the same thing). As in §14.5,
    $k = (H_1 - H_2)/(s_1 - s_2)$ and $d = (s_1 k - H_1)/r \bmod n$: the private key is public. Every signature the
    key ever made or will make must be treated as forgeable.

## What you learned

- An elliptic curve $y^2 = x^3 + ax + b$ plus a point at infinity forms a group under
  the chord-and-tangent rule; over $\mathbb{F}_p$ it is a finite group of about $p$ points.
- Scalar multiplication $dG$ is easy (double-and-add); recovering $d$ is the ECDLP,
  for which only generic $\sqrt n$ attacks are known on good curves. 256-bit curves
  give about 128-bit security.
- ECDH, ECDSA and EdDSA are Diffie-Hellman, DSA and Schnorr on curves, with the
  same strengths and the same failure modes (nonces, unchecked inputs).
- X25519 and Ed25519 were designed to make safe implementations easy: $x$-only
  ladders, twist security, complete formulas, deterministic nonces.
- P-256 is required in many standards; secp256k1 runs Bitcoin. Validate points and
  use constant-time libraries.
- Unexplained constants are a liability: Dual_EC_DRBG shows how two points can hide
  a backdoor.
- Every curve falls to Shor's algorithm; hybrids with ML-KEM are the bridge.
