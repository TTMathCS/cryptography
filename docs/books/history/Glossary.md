# Glossary

*Short definitions of the people, machines, institutions and terms of the history of secret writing, from A to Z, each with a link to the chapter and section that tells it.*

Technical terms that the Cryptanalysis book explains in full (frequency analysis, the index of coincidence, differential and linear cryptanalysis and the rest) are in its [Glossary](../cryptanalysis/Glossary.md).

## A

**Abjad**
The Arabic system that gives each letter of the alphabet a number, so that every word has a numerical value. Ciphers built on its sums, such as Ibn ad-Durayhim's letter pairs, fall to counting the sums.
See [Chapter 4](04-Baghdad-and-Cairo.md#47-numbers-and-how-much-text-is-enough).

**ADFGVX**
The German army's field cipher of 1918, designed by Fritz Nebel. A square turns each character into two letters, and a keyed transposition then shuffles those letters. Georges Painvin broke it in 1918.
See [Chapter 11](11-Radio-War-and-the-One-Time-Pad.md#114-adfgvx-and-the-man-who-broke-it-in-a-week).

**Aeneas Tacticus**
Greek writer, born about 400 BC (a guess from his command in 367 BC), who wrote a manual of defense for a city under siege about 350 BC. Its chapter 31 lists close to twenty ways to get a message through, and it is the first book on the subject.
See [Chapter 3](03-Greece-and-Rome.md#33-aeneas-tacticus-the-first-book-on-the-subject).

**AES (Advanced Encryption Standard)**
The cipher NIST chose in an open contest: fifteen candidates in 1998, five finalists in 1999, and Rijndael by Joan Daemen and Vincent Rijmen on 2 October 2000. It became FIPS 197 on 26 November 2001.
See [Chapter 18](18-The-Internet-Era.md#187-aes-a-cipher-chosen-in-public).

**Alberti, Leon Battista**
Scholar and architect (1404 to 1472) and a papal abbreviator from about 1432. In De componendis cifris, written about 1466 or 1467, he described the first European polyalphabetic cipher and set out how a cipher is attacked.
See [Chapter 6](06-Italy-Ambassadors-and-Nomenclators.md#67-alberti-and-the-disk).

**Alberti's disk**
A device of two metal disks of 24 cells each, a fixed outer ring and a turning inner one, described about 1466 or 1467. It changes the alphabet inside the message, with the setting written in the clear as a capital letter, and it separates a long-term secret (the inner ring's order) from a short-term setting.
See [Chapter 6](06-Italy-Ambassadors-and-Nomenclators.md#67-alberti-and-the-disk).

**al-Khalil ibn Ahmad**
Arabic scholar (about 718 to 786 or 791) who fixed the rules of Arabic verse and compiled its first dictionary by counting letters. Later writers credit him with the first book on cryptographic messages, now lost, and say he solved a Greek cryptogram by guessing that it opened with a standard formula.
See [Chapter 4](04-Baghdad-and-Cairo.md#43-al-khalil-and-the-first-book).

**al-Kindi**
Philosopher of Baghdad (about 801 to about 873) who wrote more than 250 works. One short treatise on deciphering, usually dated about 850, is the oldest surviving manual of codebreaking; its single known copy was found in Istanbul and published in 1987.
See [Chapter 4](04-Baghdad-and-Cairo.md#44-al-kindi-the-philosopher-of-the-arabs).

**al-Qalqashandi**
Chancery secretary (1355 to 1418) who finished his fourteen-volume encyclopedia for secretaries in Cairo in 1412. It preserved much earlier Arabic writing on ciphers, and until the 1980s it was the only source Western historians had.
See [Chapter 4](04-Baghdad-and-Cairo.md#48-damascus-and-cairo-the-mamluk-chanceries).

**Altered writing**
A scribe's bending of a script for dignity, for sacred puzzles, for a trade secret, for a cover name or as a badge of a group. Of these uses only the trade secret and the cover name keep anyone out.
See [Chapter 2](02-Egypt-Mesopotamia-and-the-Bible.md#22-khnumhotep-ii-and-the-scribe-who-changed-the-signs).

**American Black Chamber**
The US cipher office led by Herbert Yardley, begun in 1917 as MI-8 and moved to New York in 1919 as the Cipher Bureau. It read other governments' diplomatic codes, helped the American side at the Washington Naval Conference of 1921 to 1922, and was closed on 31 October 1929 by Secretary of State Stimson.
See [Chapter 11](11-Radio-War-and-the-One-Time-Pad.md#118-yardley-and-the-american-black-chamber).

**Anagram**
A sentence of scrambled letters, used in the early 1600s by Galileo, Hooke and Huygens to claim a discovery without revealing it. When the sentence was later explained, its letters could be checked, which proved the author had known the result at the time.
See [Chapter 1](01-Why-People-Hid-Writing.md#15-the-anagram-a-secret-that-proves-you-were-first).

**Apple v. FBI**
The dispute that began on 16 February 2016 with a court order that Apple help the FBI unlock one iPhone 5c after the San Bernardino shooting. It ended on 28 March 2016 when the FBI withdrew after an outside party got into the phone, and the legal question was not decided.
See [Chapter 18](18-The-Internet-Era.md#1814-the-second-crypto-war).

**Arlington Hall**
The building requisitioned by the Army in June 1942 as the base of its codebreakers. There they worked on Japanese army codes and, from 1 February 1943, on the Soviet traffic later named Venona, and most of the wartime staff were women.
See [Chapter 14](14-The-Rest-of-the-War.md#1412-arlington-hall-nebraska-avenue-and-the-women-who-did-the-work).

**Armed Forces Security Agency**
The first American attempt, created by a directive of 20 May 1949, to bring the services' codebreakers together under Rear Admiral Earl Stone. It coordinated more than it commanded, the Korean War showed its weakness, and the NSA replaced it.
See [Chapter 15](15-Shannon-and-the-Agencies.md#155-from-the-black-chamber-to-the-nsa-and-gchq).

**Arnold-André book code**
The cipher between Benedict Arnold and John André in 1779 and 1780: each word was three numbers, a page, a line and a position, in a printed book both men owned, with invisible ink in places. It was never broken; the plot failed on 23 September 1780 when André was caught with plain papers.
See [Chapter 9](09-Queens-Spies-and-Black-Chambers.md#910-arnold-andre-and-peggy-shippen).

**Arthashastra**
A Sanskrit manual of statecraft attributed to Kautilya, minister of Chandragupta Maurya. Modern scholars date its present form between about 50 and 125 AD, though the date is argued, and it is more concerned with leaks of counsel and with spies than with scripts.
See [Chapter 8](08-The-World-Beyond-Europe.md#85-india-secret-writing-as-one-of-the-sixty-four-arts).

**Atbash**
A fixed pairing of the Hebrew alphabet that swaps the first letter with the last, the second with the next to last, and so on. It appears in Jeremiah (25:26, 51:1, 51:41); Albam and Atbah are other fixed pairings, and none of the three has a key.
See [Chapter 2](02-Egypt-Mesopotamia-and-the-Bible.md#25-atbash-in-the-book-of-jeremiah).

**Autokey**
A cipher whose key is the message itself, started by a single agreed letter called the primer. Vigenère printed a true autokey in 1586; a single known plaintext letter breaks it when the primer is one letter long.
See [Chapter 7](07-Printed-Ciphers.md#79-vigenere-and-a-name-that-stuck-to-the-wrong-cipher).

## B

**Babbage, Charles**
English mathematician (1791 to 1871) who designed the Difference Engine and the Analytical Engine. In 1854 he solved the keyword cipher and left the method in notes that stayed unpublished until the twentieth century.
See [Chapter 10](10-The-Telegraph.md#104-babbage-and-the-dentists-challenge).

**Bacon, Roger**
English friar and scholar (about 1220 to about 1292) who argued for experiment and mathematics, and that secrets of nature should be written in a form the crowd could not read. He did not invent a cipher system, and he is credited with things his surviving works do not show.
See [Chapter 5](05-The-Medieval-World.md#57-secrets-of-the-schools-bacon-the-alchemists-and-a-book-about-the-stars).

**Banburismus**
Turing's method for finding the likely rotor order of naval Enigma from the ciphertexts alone, by sliding messages against each other and adding up evidence in decibans. By the usual account it is named for Banbury, where the long sheets of paper it needed were printed.
See [Chapter 13](13-Bletchley-Park.md#136-naval-enigma-banburismus-the-pinches-and-the-atlantic).

**Batey, Mavis**
Born Mavis Lever (1921 to 2013), who joined the Government Code and Cypher School in 1940 at nineteen and worked in Dilly Knox's team. She broke the Italian naval Enigma in March 1941, which gave Admiral Cunningham the Italian fleet's plan before Matapan, and the first Abwehr Enigma message in December 1941.
See [Chapter 13](13-Bletchley-Park.md#134-dilly-knox-and-his-young-women).

**Bazeries, Étienne**
French army officer (1846 to 1931) who made codebreaking his work. He read the Great Cipher in 1893 and in 1891 reinvented the wheel cipher with 20 disks.
See [Chapter 9](09-Queens-Spies-and-Black-Chambers.md#97-france-the-rossignols-and-the-great-cipher) and [Chapter 10](10-The-Telegraph.md#1011-the-wheel-cipher-jefferson-bazeries-and-de-viaris).

**Bellaso, Giovan Battista**
A secretary to cardinals from Brescia who printed the first keyword, which he called the contrasegno, in La cifra (Venice, 1553). The repeating-keyword cipher that has carried Vigenère's name since the 1800s is his.
See [Chapter 7](07-Printed-Ciphers.md#76-bellaso-and-the-countersign).

**Bernstein v. United States**
The suit filed in February 1995 in the Northern District of California to publish the source code of a cipher without a license. Judge Marilyn Hall Patel ruled in 1996 that source code is speech, Cindy Cohn led the legal team, and the case was closed in 2003 after the rules had been relaxed.
See [Chapter 17](17-Public-Keys-and-the-Crypto-Wars.md#1713-code-as-speech-the-courts-and-the-end-of-the-export-rules).

**Bertrand, Gustave**
Head of the radio-intelligence and cipher section of French military intelligence (1896 to 1976). He carried Schmidt's Enigma papers to Warsaw, attended the meeting at Pyry, and wrote Enigma (1973), the first book to describe the Polish break to a wide public.
See [Chapter 12](12-Rotors-and-Enigma.md#127-the-spy-the-french-officer-and-the-papers).

**Biham, Eli, and Shamir, Adi**
The graduate student and his adviser at the Weizmann Institute who presented differential cryptanalysis at CRYPTO in August 1990. They showed that DES's S-boxes had been built to resist it.
See [Chapter 16](16-Feistel-Lucifer-and-DES.md#166-the-s-boxes-were-stronger-not-weaker).

**Biliteral cipher**
Francis Bacon's way of writing each letter of the alphabet with two symbols in groups of five, so that a message can hide in any text whose letters take two forms. It is a hiding method, and the Friedmans' 1957 book ended the claim that one is in Shakespeare.
See [Chapter 7](07-Printed-Ciphers.md#710-bacons-two-letters).

**Bitcoin**
The electronic cash described in a paper of 31 October 2008 by a person or group using the name Satoshi Nakamoto, with the software released in January 2009. It puts old parts, hashes, signatures and proof of work, together in a ledger shared by everyone; the real identity of Nakamoto is unknown.
See [Chapter 18](18-The-Internet-Era.md#1815-money-and-other-new-uses).

**Black chamber**
A government office that routinely opened mail, copied it, resealed it and deciphered what it found. Vienna's Secret Chancery and England's Secret Department were two; the Mazzini scandal of 1844 ended the English one.
See [Chapter 9](09-Queens-Spies-and-Black-Chambers.md#98-the-black-chambers).

**Bletchley Park**
The Victorian country house about fifty miles from London where the Government Code and Cypher School moved in August 1939. By 1945 about 9,000 to 10,000 people worked there and at its outstations, roughly three quarters of them women, in huts known only by number.
See [Chapter 13](13-Bletchley-Park.md#132-a-country-house-and-a-hut-for-everything).

**Block cipher**
A cipher that turns a block of bits, such as 64 of them, into another block of the same size under a key. A good one behaves like a random shuffle of all possible blocks that can still be undone.
See [Chapter 16](16-Feistel-Lucifer-and-DES.md#163-the-feistel-network).

**Bomba**
The Polish machine designed by Rejewski in October 1938 and built by AVA in Warsaw. It joined six Enigma doubles in pairs and ran through all 17,576 positions of one rotor order to find a day's setting; the British bombe of chapter 13 was a different machine with the same name.
See [Chapter 12](12-Rotors-and-Enigma.md#129-the-race-grill-cyclometer-catalog-sheets-and-bomba).

**Bombe**
The British machine designed late in 1939 by Turing and Welchman and built at Letchworth under Harold "Doc" Keen. It tested every rotor order and position against a menu from a crib and stopped at the few that were consistent; just over 200 British and about 120 US Navy bombes were run chiefly by Wrens and WAVES.
See [Chapter 13](13-Bletchley-Park.md#135-the-crib-the-menu-and-the-bombe).

**Book code**
A code whose groups of numbers point to words in a printed book that both sides own. Arnold and André used page, line and position in 1779 and 1780.
See [Chapter 9](09-Queens-Spies-and-Black-Chambers.md#910-arnold-andre-and-peggy-shippen).

## C

**Caesar's shift**
The cipher that replaces each letter with the one a fixed number of places on. Suetonius reports a shift of three for Caesar and a shift of one for Augustus; on a 21-letter alphabet it has about twenty keys and falls to trying them all.
See [Chapter 3](03-Greece-and-Rome.md#37-rome-letters-seals-and-caesars-shift).

**Caracristi, Ann**
Codebreaker at Arlington Hall from 1942 (1921 to 2016) who worked on Japanese Army ciphers in the war and Soviet targets after it. In 1980 she became the first woman Deputy Director of the NSA.
See [Chapter 15](15-Shannon-and-the-Agencies.md#159-the-women-of-the-agencies).

**Cardano, Girolamo**
Physician and mathematician (1501 to 1576) who described the grille, a card with holes, in De subtilitate (1550), together with a first sketch of an autokey. He also wrote the Ars magna (1545) and the first work on chance.
See [Chapter 7](07-Printed-Ciphers.md#78-cardano-a-card-with-holes).

**Certificate**
A statement that binds a name to a public key so that the binding can be checked without asking anyone. Loren Kohnfelder proposed it in his MIT thesis of May 1978, and it became X.509 (1988) and the web's padlock.
See [Chapter 17](17-Public-Keys-and-the-Crypto-Wars.md#178-whose-key-is-this-certificates-and-the-first-protocol-bugs) and [Chapter 18](18-The-Internet-Era.md#185-who-vouches-for-a-key-certificates-and-the-trust-problem).

**Certificate Transparency**
The system, designed at Google by Ben Laurie, Adam Langley and Emilia Kasper and published as RFC 6962 in June 2013, that makes every certificate public. A mistake or a break-in at an authority is then found by its victims.
See [Chapter 18](18-The-Internet-Era.md#185-who-vouches-for-a-key-certificates-and-the-trust-problem).

**Champollion, Jean-François**
French scholar (1790 to 1832) who had learned Coptic as a young man and announced the reading of hieroglyphs on 27 September 1822. He worked from the Rosetta Stone and an obelisk from Philae.
See [Chapter 2](02-Egypt-Mesopotamia-and-the-Bible.md#29-when-the-script-itself-is-the-cipher-from-horapollo-to-champollion).

**Choctaw telephone squads**
About twenty Choctaw soldiers of the 142nd Infantry, 36th Division, who passed field telephone messages in their own language on 26 to 28 October 1918. It was a language and not a cipher, and it held as long as no German could speak it.
See [Chapter 11](11-Radio-War-and-the-One-Time-Pad.md#115-the-choctaw-telephone-squads).

**Cipher**
A method that works on letters, pairs of letters, syllables or bits, with a small piece of private information called the key, and turns plain text into text that looks like noise. Caesar's shift is the oldest named example in the West.
See [Chapter 1](01-Why-People-Hid-Writing.md#12-hiding-coding-and-scrambling).

**Clarke, Joan**
Cambridge mathematics graduate (1917 to 1996) recruited to Bletchley Park in June 1940 and posted to Hut 8, where she became one of the Banburismus experts. She was paid as a "linguist" because the Civil Service had no grade for a woman cryptanalyst.
See [Chapter 13](13-Bletchley-Park.md#133-three-quarters-of-the-staff).

**Clay envelope**
In the Old Assyrian trade at Kanesh (about 1900 BC), a letter or contract wrapped in a sealed case of clay that kept it whole and showed who sent it. The idea lives on in every tamper-evident seal and message authentication code.
See [Chapter 1](01-Why-People-Hid-Writing.md#13-locks-that-are-not-mathematics).

**Clipper chip**
The tamper-resistant chip announced by the White House on 16 April 1993, which used the NSA's Skipjack cipher and gave strong telephone encryption that the police could open with a warrant. Each chip's key was split between two escrow agents, and Matt Blaze's break of its 16-bit check field in 1994 showed an ordinary user could defeat the scheme; it was abandoned in the mid-1990s.
See [Chapter 17](17-Public-Keys-and-the-Crypto-Wars.md#1712-clipper-the-governments-answer).

**Code**
A method that replaces whole words, phrases or sentences with other words, numbers or symbols agreed in advance. The agreement is the key; written down it is a codebook, and unwritten it is a memory or a list of prearranged phrases.
See [Chapter 1](01-Why-People-Hid-Writing.md#12-hiding-coding-and-scrambling).

**Code book**
A list of code words or numbers for phrases, sold from 1845 so that a firm could send a sentence as one word and cut the cost of a telegram. For real secrecy a firm put a secret superencipherment on top.
See [Chapter 10](10-The-Telegraph.md#102-codes-by-the-price-of-a-word).

**Code talkers**
Soldiers who sent messages in their own languages, among them Navajo for the Marines (first recruits at Camp Pendleton in May 1942), and Comanche and Meskwaki for the Army. Their codes held because no Japanese or German spoke those languages, not because of mathematics.
See [Chapter 14](14-The-Rest-of-the-War.md#148-the-code-talkers).

**Colossus**
The electronic machine built by Tommy Flowers at the Post Office Research Station, Dollis Hill, to count the matches that revealed the chi wheel settings of the Lorenz cipher. Mark 1 was working at Bletchley Park by 5 February 1944, Mark 2 went into service on 1 June 1944, and ten were running by April 1945.
See [Chapter 13](13-Bletchley-Park.md#1311-flowers-and-colossus).

**Confusion and diffusion**
Shannon's names for the two ingredients of a practical cipher. Confusion makes the link between key and ciphertext too complicated to use; diffusion spreads each message letter over many ciphertext letters to smear away the redundancy of the language.
See [Chapter 15](15-Shannon-and-the-Agencies.md#154-what-the-1949-paper-proved).

**Copper Scroll**
Scroll 3Q15, found in Cave 3 at Qumran in 1952, a list of about sixty hiding places for treasure. Whether the treasure was real is unsettled, none of the places has been found, and the Greek letter groups after the entries are unexplained.
See [Chapter 2](02-Egypt-Mesopotamia-and-the-Bible.md#27-qumran-a-script-as-a-badge-and-a-scroll-of-treasure).

**Crib**
A piece of plaintext known or guessed to be in a message. The Greek of the Rosetta Stone was a crib for the hieroglyphs, and a guessed weather report was a crib for the bombe.
See [Chapter 13](13-Bletchley-Park.md#135-the-crib-the-menu-and-the-bombe) and [Chapter 2](02-Egypt-Mesopotamia-and-the-Bible.md#29-when-the-script-itself-is-the-cipher-from-horapollo-to-champollion).

**Cryptic A**
An alphabet of invented signs, one for each Hebrew letter, used in some of the Dead Sea scrolls. It is a plain letter-for-letter substitution, solved in the 1950s, and probably a badge for the community's leaders and teachers rather than a lock.
See [Chapter 2](02-Egypt-Mesopotamia-and-the-Bible.md#27-qumran-a-script-as-a-badge-and-a-scroll-of-treasure).

**Crypto AG**
The Swiss cipher-machine firm founded in 1952 near Zug from Boris Hagelin's earlier firms, which sold to well over a hundred countries. By the reports of 2020 the CIA and the BND owned it in secret from 1970 until the CIA sold it in 2018; some of those claims rest on single sources.
See [Chapter 15](15-Shannon-and-the-Agencies.md#156-hagelin-and-crypto-ag-a-swiss-firm-with-a-secret-owner).

**CRYPTO (conference)**
The meeting first held in August 1981 at the University of California, Santa Barbara, usually credited to David Chaum with Allen Gersho. About a hundred people came to the first, and it is held every August; the IACR now runs it.
See [Chapter 16](16-Feistel-Lucifer-and-DES.md#1611-crypto-eurocrypt-and-the-voluntary-review).

**Cryptologia**
The journal founded in January 1977 at Albion College, Michigan, for the history, technique and practice of cryptology, when there was none in English. Its back issues are a main source for the history of cryptography.
See [Chapter 16](16-Feistel-Lucifer-and-DES.md#1610-cryptography-comes-out-in-the-open).

**Culper Ring**
George Washington's spy network of 1778 to 1783, organized by Major Benjamin Tallmadge in Setauket and New York. It used a chain of messengers, a number code book and invisible stain; Townsend's role was not known until 1930.
See [Chapter 9](09-Queens-Spies-and-Black-Chambers.md#99-the-american-revolution-learning-the-trade-and-the-culper-ring).

## D

**Data Encryption Standard (DES)**
The 64-bit block cipher with a 56-bit key that grew from Lucifer at IBM. NBS proposed it on 17 March 1975, issued it as FIPS 46 in January 1977, and NIST withdrew FIPS 46-3 on 19 May 2005.
See [Chapter 16](16-Feistel-Lucifer-and-DES.md#164-the-government-asks-for-a-cipher).

**Deciban**
A tenth of a ban, the unit Turing used for weight of evidence. A ban is a factor of ten in the odds, and evidence counted in decibans can be added up as it comes.
See [Chapter 13](13-Bletchley-Park.md#136-naval-enigma-banburismus-the-pinches-and-the-atlantic).

**Deep Crack**
The key-search machine built in July 1998 by the Electronic Frontier Foundation with 1,856 custom chips, about 90 billion keys a second, for under \$250,000. It won the second RSA challenge in 56 hours and, with distributed.net, the third in 22 hours 15 minutes in January 1999.
See [Chapter 16](16-Feistel-Lucifer-and-DES.md#167-the-key-falls).

**Denning, Dorothy**
Computer scientist (born 1945) who wrote Cryptography and Data Security (1982) and, with Giovanni Sacco, found a weakness in a key-distribution protocol in 1981. In 1993 she was one of five outside reviewers of Skipjack, and her support for key escrow cost her the agreement of many colleagues.
See [Chapter 16](16-Feistel-Lucifer-and-DES.md#1612-who-was-in-the-room) and [Chapter 17](17-Public-Keys-and-the-Crypto-Wars.md#1712-clipper-the-governments-answer).

**Denniston, Alastair**
Born in 1881, he led the Government Code and Cypher School from the interwar years until February 1942, hired mathematicians as well as linguists, and led the British delegation to Pyry in July 1939. He then ran its diplomatic section and retired in 1945.
See [Chapter 13](13-Bletchley-Park.md#132-a-country-house-and-a-hut-for-everything).

**Depth**
Two or more messages enciphered with the same key stream. It is the mistake of reusing a one-time pad, and it gave Tiltman his break of Lorenz on 30 August 1941.
See [Chapter 13](13-Bletchley-Park.md#139-tunny-a-depth-a-rectangle-and-a-machine-nobody-had-seen).

**Diagonal board**
Gordon Welchman's addition to the bombe, a 26-by-26 plug field that used the plugboard's symmetry (if A is plugged to B then B is plugged to A). It cut the false stops by a large factor.
See [Chapter 13](13-Bletchley-Park.md#135-the-crib-the-menu-and-the-bombe).

**Differential cryptanalysis**
An attack that follows how a chosen difference between two plaintexts spreads through the rounds. IBM's team found it in 1974 and kept it secret; Biham and Shamir presented it in 1990.
See [Chapter 16](16-Feistel-Lucifer-and-DES.md#166-the-s-boxes-were-stronger-not-weaker).

**Diffie-Hellman key exchange**
The 1976 method by which two strangers agree on a key over a line everyone can hear, with a gap between the work of the users and the work of the attacker that grows exponentially. Malcolm Williamson at GCHQ had found an exponentiation key agreement in January 1974.
See [Chapter 17](17-Public-Keys-and-the-Crypto-Wars.md#173-diffie-and-hellman-new-directions).

**Diffie, Whitfield, and Hellman, Martin**
The Stanford pair (Diffie born 1944, Hellman 1945) whose paper "New Directions in Cryptography" of 1976 gave a key exchange, defined the digital signature and asked for a trapdoor function. They also made the 1977 estimate of what a machine to search the DES key would cost, and shared the Turing Award in 2015.
See [Chapter 17](17-Public-Keys-and-the-Crypto-Wars.md#173-diffie-and-hellman-new-directions).

**DigiNotar**
A Dutch certificate authority, owned by Vasco, that issued certificates until 2011, among them for the Dutch government. Its roots were removed from browsers in September 2011 and it went bankrupt on 20 September 2011.
See [Chapter 18](18-The-Internet-Era.md#185-who-vouches-for-a-key-certificates-and-the-trust-problem).

**Driscoll, Agnes Meyer**
Navy codebreaker (1889 to 1971), a civilian after she joined as a yeoman (F) in June 1918, who trained the Navy's first cryptanalysts and worked on the Red Book and Blue Book codes, the Red machine and the first attack on JN-25. Her credit against Safford's and Rochefort's is disputed.
See [Chapter 14](14-The-Rest-of-the-War.md#142-agnes-meyer-driscoll-and-the-navys-first-codebreakers).

**Dual_EC_DRBG**
A random-bit generator built from an elliptic curve, in NIST's SP 800-90 of 2006 and pushed by the NSA. Shumow and Ferguson showed in August 2007 that whoever chose its two fixed points could know a secret number that predicts its output, and the Snowden documents of 2013 linked it to the NSA; NIST advised against it in September 2013 and removed it in 2015.
See [Chapter 18](18-The-Internet-Era.md#1812-snowden-and-dual_ec_drbg).

## E

**Elliptic-curve cryptography**
Diffie-Hellman and signatures done on the points of an elliptic curve, proposed independently in 1985 by Neal Koblitz (born 1948) and Victor Miller (born 1947). Curves of 256 bits do the work of RSA keys of 3072 bits.
See [Chapter 17](17-Public-Keys-and-the-Crypto-Wars.md#179-more-trapdoors-a-curve-and-a-wall-of-patents).

**Enigma**
The rotor cipher machine patented by Arthur Scherbius in 1918 and sold commercially from 1923. The navy adopted it in 1926, the army in 1928 and the air force after 1935, and Poland read it from 1933 and then Britain and its allies.
See [Chapter 12](12-Rotors-and-Enigma.md#123-enigma-from-a-postal-congress-to-the-wehrmacht).

**Enigmatic writing**
Egyptian texts written in puzzle signs, as in the royal tombs of the New Kingdom and the temple at Esna, to make sacred texts a puzzle for the learned. Whether the aim was learning or keeping out the profane is inference.
See [Chapter 2](02-Egypt-Mesopotamia-and-the-Bible.md#23-enigmatic-writing-the-priests-puzzle).

## F

**Feistel, Horst**
German-born IBM engineer (30 January 1915 to 14 November 1990) who led the group that made Lucifer from 1968. His 1973 Scientific American article taught the principle to the public.
See [Chapter 16](16-Feistel-Lucifer-and-DES.md#162-horst-feistel-and-lucifer).

**Feistel network**
A round sends the right half across unchanged and XORs a function of it and a round key into the left half, and the halves then change places. It can be undone whatever the function is, by running the same rounds with the keys reversed.
See [Chapter 16](16-Feistel-Lucifer-and-DES.md#163-the-feistel-network).

**Fialka**
The Soviet M-125 rotor machine, in service from about 1956, with ten rotors of 30 contacts. The Warsaw Pact used one design with national rotor wirings, and it served until the early 1990s.
See [Chapter 15](15-Shannon-and-the-Agencies.md#157-the-soviet-side-fialka-and-the-east-german-machines).

**Flowers, Tommy**
Post Office telephone engineer (1905 to 1998) who believed a large machine could be built from thousands of valves and kept running. He built Colossus and was bound by the Official Secrets Act until the 1970s.
See [Chapter 13](13-Bletchley-Park.md#1311-flowers-and-colossus).

**Frequency analysis**
Counting the symbols of a cryptogram, comparing the count with one from ordinary text, and correcting with letter combinations and probable words. Al-Kindi wrote it down about 850.
See [Chapter 4](04-Baghdad-and-Cairo.md#45-the-treatise-counting-letters).

**Friedman, Elizebeth Smith**
Codebreaker (26 August 1892 to 31 October 1980) hired in 1916 to look for ciphers in Shakespeare. She led the Coast Guard's code unit against the rum-runners and in the war ran a unit against German spy rings in South America; her work was told in full only after her death.
See [Chapter 11](11-Radio-War-and-the-One-Time-Pad.md#119-elizebeth-friedman-and-the-rum-runners).

**Friedman, William F.**
Geneticist who became the United States' chief cryptanalyst (24 September 1891 to 2 November 1969). He made the index of coincidence in 1920, founded the Signal Intelligence Service in 1930 and directed the break of Purple.
See [Chapter 11](11-Radio-War-and-the-One-Time-Pad.md#1110-william-friedmans-index-of-coincidence).

## G

**Gallows letter**
Mary, Queen of Scots' cipher reply to Babington of 17 July 1586, to which Phelippes added a forged postscript. The original is lost, the copy survives, and how far Phelippes altered it is still argued.
See [Chapter 9](09-Queens-Spies-and-Black-Chambers.md#94-the-gallows-letter-and-the-trial).

**GC&CS**
The Government Code and Cypher School, formed in 1919 from the Admiralty's Room 40 and the army's MI1(b) and moved to Bletchley Park in August 1939. It was renamed GCHQ in 1946.
See [Chapter 13](13-Bletchley-Park.md#132-a-country-house-and-a-hut-for-everything).

**GCHQ**
The Government Communications Headquarters, the British signals-intelligence agency, which took that name in 1946 from the Government Code and Cypher School. Through the UKUSA Agreement it is tied to the NSA.
See [Chapter 15](15-Shannon-and-the-Agencies.md#155-from-the-black-chamber-to-the-nsa-and-gchq).

**Gematria**
Turning a name into a number by adding the values of its letters. It is a riddle and not a lock; the best-known case is 666 in Revelation, read as Nero Caesar written in Hebrew letters.
See [Chapter 2](02-Egypt-Mesopotamia-and-the-Bible.md#26-letters-that-are-numbers-gematria-cover-names-and-a-code-that-was-not).

**Goldwasser, Shafi**
Computer scientist (born 1958) who, with Silvio Micali, defined what it means for encryption to be secure (probabilistic encryption and semantic security, 1982 and 1984) and, with Micali and Charles Rackoff, introduced zero-knowledge proofs in 1985. They shared the Turing Award for 2012.
See [Chapter 17](17-Public-Keys-and-the-Crypto-Wars.md#179-more-trapdoors-a-curve-and-a-wall-of-patents).

**Grabeel, Gene**
Virginia home-economics teacher (1920 to 2015) who became the first analyst of the Soviet traffic that began on 1 February 1943 and was later named Venona.
See [Chapter 14](14-The-Rest-of-the-War.md#1413-venona-i-the-pad-printed-twice).

**Great Cipher**
The nomenclator made in the 1670s by Antoine Rossignol and his son Bonaventure for the letters of Louis XIV. It held for about two centuries until Étienne Bazeries read it in 1893.
See [Chapter 9](09-Queens-Spies-and-Black-Chambers.md#97-france-the-rossignols-and-the-great-cipher).

**Grill**
Rejewski's first daily method, from 1933, a paper method that tested the products from the indicators against what the machine could do. It relied on the plugboard having few cables and got harder as more were used.
See [Chapter 12](12-Rotors-and-Enigma.md#129-the-race-grill-cyclometer-catalog-sheets-and-bomba).

**Grille**
A card with holes, described by Cardano in 1550, laid over a sheet so that the message is written through the holes and the rest filled in with cover text.
See [Chapter 7](07-Printed-Ciphers.md#78-cardano-a-card-with-holes).

**Grotjan, Genevieve**
Junior cryptanalyst in the Army's Signal Intelligence Service (born 1913, later Genevieve Grotjan Feinstein, died 2006). On 20 September 1940 she saw a recurrence of letter pairs that showed how Purple's twenties were stepped.
See [Chapter 14](14-The-Rest-of-the-War.md#144-genevieve-grotjan-and-the-break-of-purple).

**Grover's algorithm**
Lov Grover's quantum search of 1996, which finds the one key out of N that passes a test in about the square root of N steps. A 128-bit key then costs about 2^64 steps that must run one after another, so symmetric ciphers survive with longer keys.
See [Chapter 19](19-Hardware-Quantum-and-the-Long-View.md#198-a-computer-made-of-physics-feynman-to-shor-and-grover).

## H

**Hagelin, Boris**
Engineer (2 July 1892 to 7 September 1983) who built small, reliable cipher machines, among them the C-38 and its American form, the M-209. His Swiss firm became Crypto AG.
See [Chapter 15](15-Shannon-and-the-Agencies.md#156-hagelin-and-crypto-ag-a-swiss-firm-with-a-secret-owner).

**Hardware security module (HSM)**
A tamper-resistant box that holds keys and does the cryptography itself, built to erase its keys if someone opens it. Mohamed Atalla built the first one the chapter can find in the early 1970s (dated 1972 or 1973).
See [Chapter 19](19-Hardware-Quantum-and-the-Long-View.md#193-keys-in-boxes-and-cards-hsms-and-smart-cards).

**Heartbleed**
The bug announced on 7 April 2014 (CVE-2014-0160) in OpenSSL's TLS heartbeat code, which trusted the length in a request and sent back up to 64 kilobytes of server memory. That memory could hold passwords, session cookies and sometimes a server's private key.
See [Chapter 18](18-The-Internet-Era.md#1811-three-famous-bugs).

**Hebern, Edward**
American inventor (died 1952) of an electric typewriter that enciphered as you type, with wired rotors; he applied for a US patent in 1921. The Navy bought some machines, and his company failed in the mid-1920s.
See [Chapter 12](12-Rotors-and-Enigma.md#122-four-inventors-in-four-countries).

**Herodotus**
Greek historian (about 484 BC to probably the 420s BC) whose Histories hold the oldest surviving stories of hidden messages: the tattooed slave, the blank tablet, the hare and the arrows.
See [Chapter 3](03-Greece-and-Rome.md#32-hidden-messages-the-stories-in-herodotus).

**Hildegard of Bingen**
Abbess (1098 to 17 September 1179) who made an alphabet of unknown letters and a vocabulary of an invented language. Why, no source says; she was named a Doctor of the Church in 2012.
See [Chapter 5](05-The-Medieval-World.md#56-hildegards-unknown-letters).

**Homophones**
Several signs for one common letter, used in turn to flatten the count of single signs. The earliest case cited is a Mantuan key of 1401 that gave each vowel several signs.
See [Chapter 6](06-Italy-Ambassadors-and-Nomenclators.md#64-homophones-and-nulls-the-mantuan-key-of-1401).

**Huts**
The numbered wooden buildings on the lawns of Bletchley Park, so named that no one could tell from a hut number what was done inside. Hut 6 broke army and air force Enigma, Hut 8 naval Enigma, and Huts 3 and 4 turned decrypts into intelligence.
See [Chapter 13](13-Bletchley-Park.md#132-a-country-house-and-a-hut-for-everything).

**Hybrid key exchange**
A key exchange that runs X25519 and a post-quantum KEM side by side and combines both secrets, so an attacker must break both. OpenSSH 9.0 (April 2022) made one its default, and Chrome switched on X25519MLKEM768 in November 2024.
See [Chapter 19](19-Hardware-Quantum-and-the-Long-View.md#1912-hybrids-deadlines-and-what-is-still-open).

**Hygeburg**
An English nun at Heidenheim in Bavaria in the 700s who wrote the lives of Willibald and Wynnebald and signed them in a vowel cipher. Bernhard Bischoff read her name in 1931.
See [Chapter 5](05-The-Medieval-World.md#52-the-scribe-signs-his-name).

## I

**IACR**
The International Association for Cryptologic Research, formed in 1982 by participants at CRYPTO and Eurocrypt. It runs the main meetings, publishes the Journal of Cryptology and keeps the ePrint archive.
See [Chapter 16](16-Feistel-Lucifer-and-DES.md#1611-crypto-eurocrypt-and-the-voluntary-review).

**Ibn ad-Durayhim**
Writer born in Mosul (1312 to 1361) whose Keys to the Treasures describes many cipher systems, among them letter pairs by sums and several spellings for one letter. It also shows the solving of a real cryptogram, and al-Qalqashandi quotes it.
See [Chapter 4](04-Baghdad-and-Cairo.md#48-damascus-and-cairo-the-mamluk-chanceries).

**Ibn 'Adlan**
Scholar (1187 to 1268), probably of Damascus, whose treatise for the ruler al-Ashraf, written before 1237, gives tables of common short words and the first surviving statement of how long a cryptogram must be. His figure of 90 letters is a sound rule of thumb for counting.
See [Chapter 4](04-Baghdad-and-Cairo.md#47-numbers-and-how-much-text-is-enough).

**Index of coincidence**
William Friedman's measure of 1920 of how likely two letters drawn from a text are to match. It is higher for ordinary language than for random letters, which made it a tool for measuring polyalphabetic ciphers.
See [Chapter 11](11-Radio-War-and-the-One-Time-Pad.md#1110-william-friedmans-index-of-coincidence).

**Indicator**
In Enigma, the message key typed twice (LUFLUF in the chapter's example) and sent at the head of the message. The repetition gave the Poles a relation between the first and fourth letters, the second and fifth, and the third and sixth.
See [Chapter 12](12-Rotors-and-Enigma.md#125-the-daily-key-and-the-message).

**ITAR and the Munitions List**
The International Traffic in Arms Regulations, which under the Arms Export Control Act of 1976 listed cryptographic devices, software and technical data in Category XIII of the US Munitions List. Export needed a State Department license on the NSA's advice, a 1992 agreement gave quick approval to RC2 and RC4 products with 40-bit keys, and control moved to the Commerce Department at the end of 1996.
See [Chapter 17](17-Public-Keys-and-the-Crypto-Wars.md#1710-cryptography-as-a-munition-the-export-rules).

## J

**JN-25**
The Japanese navy's five-digit code with an additive on top, introduced in June 1939; the Americans numbered it the twenty-fifth Japanese navy system they had catalogued. Station HYPO read enough of it in May 1942 to find Midway.
See [Chapter 14](14-The-Rest-of-the-War.md#142-agnes-meyer-driscoll-and-the-navys-first-codebreakers) and [Chapter 14](14-The-Rest-of-the-War.md#146-station-hypo-jn-25-and-midway).

## K

**Kahn, David**
Journalist and historian (1930 to 2024) whose The Codebreakers (1967) made the history of cryptology a field of its own. It remains the standard reference, though declassified documents have overtaken its account of the Second World War.
See [Chapter 1](01-Why-People-Hid-Writing.md#17-how-we-know-anything).

**Kasiski examination**
Finding the length of a repeating key from the distances between repeated groups of letters in the ciphertext. Babbage did it in 1854, and Friedrich Kasiski published the method in Berlin in 1863.
See [Chapter 10](10-The-Telegraph.md#105-kasiski-and-the-fall-of-the-keyword).

**Kerckhoffs, Auguste**
Professor of German in Paris (19 January 1835 to 9 August 1903) who wrote La cryptographie militaire (1883), which set out six requirements for a field cipher.
See [Chapter 10](10-The-Telegraph.md#1012-kerckhoffs-and-the-six-rules).

**Kerckhoffs's principle**
The second of his six rules: a system must not require secrecy and must be able to fall into the enemy's hands without harm. Modern cryptography follows it.
See [Chapter 10](10-The-Telegraph.md#1012-kerckhoffs-and-the-six-rules).

**Key sheet**
The daily list every unit on an Enigma net held, printed in Berlin and kept under guard. It gave the rotor order, the ring settings, the plugboard pairs and, in the early years, a ground setting.
See [Chapter 12](12-Rotors-and-Enigma.md#125-the-daily-key-and-the-message).

**Khipu**
The knotted cord the Andes used for accounts and counts, and perhaps for other records. About a thousand survive, the numbers can mostly be read, and whether any cord records words is argued; it is a decipherment problem and not a cipher.
See [Chapter 8](08-The-World-Beyond-Europe.md#87-the-andes-and-the-maya-writing-that-is-not-secret).

**Khnumhotep II**
Egyptian nome governor of the 12th Dynasty (about 1900 BC), whose tomb at Beni Hasan records a scribe changing signs in his autobiography. Kahn's Codebreakers begins with it.
See [Chapter 2](02-Egypt-Mesopotamia-and-the-Bible.md#22-khnumhotep-ii-and-the-scribe-who-changed-the-signs).

**Knox, Dilly**
Cambridge classicist (23 July 1884 to 27 February 1943) who broke the Enigma of the Spanish Civil War in 1937 and led the small team that broke the Italian naval Enigma and the Abwehr Enigma.
See [Chapter 13](13-Bletchley-Park.md#134-dilly-knox-and-his-young-women).

## L

**Lavinde's key**
The oldest surviving European nomenclator, made in 1379 by Gabriele de Lavinde of Parma for Pope Clement VII at Avignon, now in the Vatican archives. It shows a cipher alphabet with code symbols for common names already complete.
See [Chapter 6](06-Italy-Ambassadors-and-Nomenclators.md#63-the-nomenclator-a-cipher-with-a-list-of-names).

**Letterlocking**
Folding and cutting a sheet of paper so that it is its own envelope and its own tamper alarm. It was used from the 1400s to the 1800s and ended when the gummed envelope came in.
See [Chapter 1](01-Why-People-Hid-Writing.md#13-locks-that-are-not-mathematics).

**Linear cryptanalysis**
Matsui's attack of 1993, which looks for a linear relation among plaintext, ciphertext and key bits that holds a little more than half the time. Matsui ran it on DES in 1994.
See [Chapter 16](16-Feistel-Lucifer-and-DES.md#166-the-s-boxes-were-stronger-not-weaker).

**Lorenz SZ40/42**
The German teleprinter cipher attachment of the high command, used from 1940 as the SZ40 and 1942 as the SZ42, with twelve wheels and 501 pins. It added its key to each five-bit character by XOR, and the British called it Tunny.
See [Chapter 13](13-Bletchley-Park.md#139-tunny-a-depth-a-rectangle-and-a-machine-nobody-had-seen).

**Lucifer**
The name of several cipher designs made by Feistel's IBM group between about 1969 and 1974. One was in a cash dispenser system for Lloyds Bank about 1971 or 1972, and the cipher was the base of DES.
See [Chapter 16](16-Feistel-Lucifer-and-DES.md#162-horst-feistel-and-lucifer).

## M

**M-209**
The Hagelin pin-wheel machine the size of a lunch box, about 140,000 of which were built for the US Army. Its messages only had to stay secret for a few hours, and the Germans could break it with enough traffic.
See [Chapter 13](13-Bletchley-Park.md#1313-the-machines-that-held).

**Magic**
The name for the decrypts of Japan's diplomatic traffic from the autumn of 1940, first meaning Purple and then everything read of the foreign ministry. It went to a very short list of readers.
See [Chapter 14](14-The-Rest-of-the-War.md#145-magic-what-was-read-and-what-it-changed).

**Mary, Queen of Scots**
Born 8 December 1542, she was a prisoner in England from 1568. She was found guilty of consenting to a plot against Elizabeth and beheaded at Fotheringhay on 8 February 1587.
See [Chapter 9](09-Queens-Spies-and-Black-Chambers.md#92-mary-queen-of-scots-and-the-barrel-at-chartley).

**Menu**
The chain of links between plain and cipher letters that a crib gives, drawn so that a bombe can be wired to match it.
See [Chapter 13](13-Bletchley-Park.md#135-the-crib-the-menu-and-the-bombe).

**Merkle's puzzles**
Ralph Merkle's method of 1974 (published 1978) by which two strangers can agree on a key in public, though the attacker's work grows only as the square of the users'. It began as a term-project proposal for a Berkeley course, was never put into service, and led to Diffie and Hellman's exponential gap.
See [Chapter 17](17-Public-Keys-and-the-Crypto-Wars.md#172-merkles-puzzles-a-rejected-term-paper).

**Message key**
The three letters an Enigma operator chose for each message to say where in the 17,576 positions to start. It meant that the day's long-term key was never used the same way twice.
See [Chapter 12](12-Rotors-and-Enigma.md#125-the-daily-key-and-the-message).

## N

**National Security Agency**
The American agency created by Truman's memorandum of 24 October 1952 and counted from 4 November, which combined signals intelligence and communications security under the Secretary of Defense. It had a public role in DES that is still argued.
See [Chapter 15](15-Shannon-and-the-Agencies.md#155-from-the-black-chamber-to-the-nsa-and-gchq).

**Newmanry**
The section named for Max Newman, begun at the end of 1942, that ran Heath Robinson and then Colossus on the Lorenz cipher. By the end of the war it had 272 Wrens and 27 men.
See [Chapter 13](13-Bletchley-Park.md#1310-the-testery-the-newmanry-and-the-wrens-at-the-colossus).

**Nihilist cipher**
A hand cipher of Russian radicals in the 1880s: a Polybius square with a keyword turns the message into two-digit numbers, and a second keyword's numbers are added on top. It falls to the same steps as any repeating-key cipher.
See [Chapter 10](10-The-Telegraph.md#109-russia-the-okhrana-and-the-nihilist-cipher).

**Nomenclator**
A cipher alphabet together with a code list that gives a single sign to common names, titles and words. It carried the diplomatic secrets of Europe from Avignon in 1379 to about 1800.
See [Chapter 6](06-Italy-Ambassadors-and-Nomenclators.md#63-the-nomenclator-a-cipher-with-a-list-of-names).

**Non-secret encryption**
The public-key idea found in secret at GCHQ: James Ellis in January 1970, Clifford Cocks's RSA-like scheme on 20 November 1973, and Malcolm Williamson's key agreement in January 1974. It was announced in December 1997, weeks after Ellis died on 25 November 1997, and nothing released shows signatures.
See [Chapter 17](17-Public-Keys-and-the-Crypto-Wars.md#176-the-secret-that-was-found-twice).

**Nsibidi**
A system of signs of the Cross River region of southeastern Nigeria and western Cameroon, cut, painted or made with the hands. Some signs were reserved to members of the Ekpe society, and related signs reached Cuba in the Abakuá society.
See [Chapter 1](01-Why-People-Hid-Writing.md#13-locks-that-are-not-mathematics) and [Chapter 8](08-The-World-Beyond-Europe.md#88-west-africa-and-ethiopia-membership-and-double-meaning).

**Null**
A sign that means nothing, scattered through a text to break up word lengths and repeats.
See [Chapter 6](06-Italy-Ambassadors-and-Nomenclators.md#64-homophones-and-nulls-the-mantuan-key-of-1401).

**Nüshu**
"Women's writing", a phonetic script of a few hundred slanted characters used by women in Jiangyong, Hunan, to write the local dialect. What kept it private was men's lack of interest and not a hiding method.
See [Chapter 1](01-Why-People-Hid-Writing.md#13-locks-that-are-not-mathematics).

## O

**Ogham**
The early Irish script of strokes along a line, carved on stones from the 4th to the 7th centuries. A school tract copied in the Book of Ballymote about 1390 lists about a hundred secret forms of it, such as Ogam Bricrenn, which gives each letter a count of dots.
See [Chapter 5](05-The-Medieval-World.md#55-ogham-and-the-irish-love-of-the-hidden).

**Okhrana**
The Russian political police formed in 1881 after the killing of Alexander II. It read mail, telegrams and the ciphers of revolutionaries until it was closed after the revolution of March 1917.
See [Chapter 10](10-The-Telegraph.md#109-russia-the-okhrana-and-the-nihilist-cipher).

**One-time pad**
A cipher whose key is random, as long as the message and used once. Vernam and Mauborgne share the credit with earlier and independent inventors, and every real break of a pad has come from reuse or poor randomness.
See [Chapter 11](11-Radio-War-and-the-One-Time-Pad.md#117-vernam-mauborgne-and-the-cipher-that-cannot-be-broken).

**Operation Vula**
The African National Congress's communications system of 1988 to 1990 that moved encrypted messages between its underground inside South Africa and Lusaka by laptop, tape and public telephone, with random keys carried on floppy disks. Tim Jenkin designed it, and it was uncovered in 1990.
See [Chapter 17](17-Public-Keys-and-the-Crypto-Wars.md#1714-operation-vula-the-laptop-the-tape-and-the-telephone).

## P

**Painvin, Georges**
French paleontologist (1886 to 1980) turned cryptanalyst, who broke ADFGX and ADFGVX in 1918. His solution of 2 June helped the French meet the German attack at Compiègne.
See [Chapter 11](11-Radio-War-and-the-One-Time-Pad.md#114-adfgvx-and-the-man-who-broke-it-in-a-week).

**Perfect secrecy**
Shannon's definition of a cipher whose ciphertext tells an attacker nothing about the message. He proved the one-time pad has it and that any system that has it needs at least as many equally likely keys as messages.
See [Chapter 15](15-Shannon-and-the-Agencies.md#154-what-the-1949-paper-proved).

**PGP**
Pretty Good Privacy, the email program Philip Zimmermann (born 1954) released in June 1991. It used RSA and a symmetric cipher and a web of trust; he was investigated by a grand jury from 1993 and never charged, and the case was dropped in January 1996.
See [Chapter 17](17-Public-Keys-and-the-Crypto-Wars.md#1711-pretty-good-privacy).

**Phelippes, Thomas**
Walsingham's decipherer (about 1556 to about 1625), who read Mary's letters and forged a postscript to the gallows letter. His later career went badly, with imprisonment for debt and for dealings with Catholic exiles.
See [Chapter 9](09-Queens-Spies-and-Black-Chambers.md#92-mary-queen-of-scots-and-the-barrel-at-chartley).

**Pinches**
The captures of German naval Enigma material: the trawler Krebs in March 1941, the weather ship München in May, and the U-boats U-110 in May 1941 and U-559 in October 1942. They gave Hut 8 the codebooks it needed.
See [Chapter 13](13-Bletchley-Park.md#136-naval-enigma-banburismus-the-pinches-and-the-atlantic).

**Playfair cipher**
A pair cipher on a 5-by-5 square invented by Charles Wheatstone in 1854 and pressed on the government by Lyon Playfair, whose name it carries. The British Army used it in the field from the Boer War to the First World War.
See [Chapter 10](10-The-Telegraph.md#106-wheatstone-playfair-and-the-pair-cipher).

**Plugboard**
The front panel of the army Enigma, added about 1930, whose cables swap pairs of letters before and after the rotors. It supplied almost all the machine's key space.
See [Chapter 12](12-Rotors-and-Enigma.md#124-inside-the-machine).

**Poe, Edgar Allan**
American writer whose cryptogram challenges of 1839 to 1841 and the story "The Gold-Bug" (1843) made substitution ciphers a public pastime. Legrand's solution in the story is al-Kindi's method told again.
See [Chapter 10](10-The-Telegraph.md#103-poe-and-the-cryptogram-craze).

**Polyalphabetic cipher**
A cipher that uses more than one cipher alphabet and changes among them inside the message. Alberti's disk is the first European one.
See [Chapter 6](06-Italy-Ambassadors-and-Nomenclators.md#67-alberti-and-the-disk).

**Polybius**
Greek historian (about 200 BC to about 118 BC) born in Megalopolis whose Book 10 describes a torch-signal system and the Roman camp's watchword.
See [Chapter 3](03-Greece-and-Rome.md#35-polybiuss-square).

**Polybius's square**
A grid of letters in five rows of five, so that each letter becomes two numbers from one to five. It was built for torch signals and is the root of tap codes, the Nihilist cipher, Playfair and ADFGVX.
See [Chapter 3](03-Greece-and-Rome.md#35-polybiuss-square).

**Porta, Giambattista della**
Naturalist of Naples (about 1535 to 4 February 1615) whose De furtivis literarum notis (1563) printed a reciprocal table of eleven alphabets, in which encrypting and decrypting are the same act, and a digraphic cipher of 400 symbols.
See [Chapter 7](07-Printed-Ciphers.md#77-porta-a-table-that-undoes-itself).

**Post-quantum cryptography**
Public-key algorithms meant to resist a quantum computer. NIST's process, begun with a call in December 2016, published FIPS 203 (ML-KEM), FIPS 204 (ML-DSA) and FIPS 205 (SLH-DSA) on 13 August 2024.
See [Chapter 19](19-Hardware-Quantum-and-the-Long-View.md#1910-the-nist-competition-2016-to-2025).

**Probable word**
A word likely to occur in a message, such as a standard opening. It is the oldest form of the crib, and al-Khalil is said to have used one.
See [Chapter 4](04-Baghdad-and-Cairo.md#45-the-treatise-counting-letters).

**Purple**
The Japanese foreign ministry's machine of February 1939, the Type 97 Alphabetical Typewriter, which used stepping switches and split the alphabet into sixes and twenties. The Army team under Frank Rowlett broke the twenties on 20 September 1940.
See [Chapter 14](14-The-Rest-of-the-War.md#144-genevieve-grotjan-and-the-break-of-purple).

**Pyry**
The Polish Cipher Bureau's secret center in the Kabaty forest south of Warsaw, where on 25 and 26 July 1939 the Poles showed French and British delegations the whole of their Enigma work.
See [Chapter 12](12-Rotors-and-Enigma.md#1210-pyry-july-1939).

## Q

**Quantum computer**
A computer built from quantum parts, imagined by Richard Feynman in 1981 and described precisely by David Deutsch in 1985. Its unit is the qubit; no machine has factored more than a classroom example.
See [Chapter 19](19-Hardware-Quantum-and-the-Long-View.md#198-a-computer-made-of-physics-feynman-to-shor-and-grover).

**Quantum key distribution (BB84)**
A way to agree on a key whose secrecy rests on physics, described by Charles Bennett and Gilles Brassard in 1984 and built on Stephen Wiesner's idea. It works in the laboratory, and the agencies prefer post-quantum algorithms to it for national secrets.
See [Chapter 19](19-Hardware-Quantum-and-the-Long-View.md#1911-other-nations-and-the-quantum-link).

## R

**Red**
The Japanese foreign ministry's cipher machine of about 1931, which enciphered the six vowels (the sixes) and the twenty consonants separately. That left the skeleton of every word in view and was the weakness the Americans used.
See [Chapter 14](14-The-Rest-of-the-War.md#143-red-sixes-and-twenties-the-armys-japanese-problem).

**Reflector**
The wheel at the far end of Enigma's rotors that sends the current back by a different path, patented by Willi Korn in 1926. It makes the machine its own inverse and means that no letter can ever encrypt to itself.
See [Chapter 12](12-Rotors-and-Enigma.md#124-inside-the-machine).

**Rejewski, Marian**
Polish mathematician (1905 to 13 February 1980) who found the wiring and the daily keys of the army Enigma. His central fact was that the cycle lengths of the products of the indicator permutations do not depend on the plugboard, and he had the wiring by December 1932.
See [Chapter 12](12-Rotors-and-Enigma.md#128-rejewskis-break).

**Riverbank**
Colonel George Fabyan's estate at Geneva, Illinois, where a cipher department, begun around 1912 to 1915, first sought a Baconian cipher in Shakespeare. William Friedman and Elizebeth Smith worked there and became the founders of American codebreaking.
See [Chapter 11](11-Radio-War-and-the-One-Time-Pad.md#116-riverbank-the-friedmans-and-the-bacon-shakespeare-ciphers).

**Rochefort, Joseph**
Naval officer (12 May 1900 to 20 July 1976) who took command of Station HYPO in June 1941. His estimates led Nimitz to put his carriers in position for Midway.
See [Chapter 14](14-The-Rest-of-the-War.md#146-station-hypo-jn-25-and-midway).

**Rök runestone**
The longest runic inscription of the Viking Age, at Rök in Östergötland, Sweden, carved in the 800s by or for a man named Varin in memory of his son Vämod. Part of it is in cipher runes, in which one rune replaces another along the rune row.
See [Chapter 5](05-The-Medieval-World.md#54-runes-that-hide-inside-runes).

**Room 40**
The British Admiralty's codebreaking office, begun in August 1914 under Sir Alfred Ewing and run by Captain W. R. Hall from the autumn. It read the German navy from captured code books and the Zimmermann telegram, and in 1919 became part of the Government Code and Cypher School.
See [Chapter 11](11-Radio-War-and-the-One-Time-Pad.md#112-room-40).

**Rosetta Stone**
A decree of Ptolemy V carved in 196 BC at Memphis in hieroglyphs, Demotic and Greek, and found in July 1799 near Rashid in the Nile delta. Its Greek text served as a crib for the hieroglyphs.
See [Chapter 2](02-Egypt-Mesopotamia-and-the-Bible.md#29-when-the-script-itself-is-the-cipher-from-horapollo-to-champollion).

**Rossignol, Antoine**
Frenchman from Albi (1600 to 1682) who took Réalmont in 1626 by reading an intercepted letter. He founded a family cipher office that served the French crown for a century, and his son Bonaventure took it over.
See [Chapter 9](09-Queens-Spies-and-Black-Chambers.md#97-france-the-rossignols-and-the-great-cipher).

**Rotor**
A wheel wired inside as a scrambled alphabet that turns a little after every letter. Four inventors in four countries arrived at it between 1915 and 1919, and three rotors give a key that does not repeat for about seventeen thousand letters.
See [Chapter 12](12-Rotors-and-Enigma.md#122-four-inventors-in-four-countries).

**Rowlett, Frank**
Virginia schoolteacher who joined Friedman in 1930 and led the Army section that broke Purple's twenties. With Friedman he designed SIGABA, to which he contributed its fifteen-rotor design.
See [Chapter 14](14-The-Rest-of-the-War.md#143-red-sixes-and-twenties-the-armys-japanese-problem).

**Różycki, Jerzy**
Polish mathematician (1909 to 9 January 1942) who devised the clock method for finding the right-hand rotor. He drowned at 32 when the ship Lamoricière sank in the Mediterranean.
See [Chapter 12](12-Rotors-and-Enigma.md#1211-what-became-of-them).

**RSA**
The first practical public-key encryption and signature scheme, worked out at MIT in April 1977 by Ron Rivest (born 1947), Adi Shamir (1952) and Leonard Adleman (1945), whose security rests on the difficulty of factoring. The patent ran out in September 2000, and they won the Turing Award in 2002.
See [Chapter 17](17-Public-Keys-and-the-Crypto-Wars.md#174-rivest-shamir-and-adleman-the-lock-that-opens-one-way).

**RSA-129**
The 129-digit challenge number printed in Martin Gardner's column in 1977. Its factorization was announced on 26 April 1994 by Atkins, Graff, Lenstra and Leyland with about 600 volunteers, and the message under it read THE MAGIC WORDS ARE SQUEAMISH OSSIFRAGE.
See [Chapter 17](17-Public-Keys-and-the-Crypto-Wars.md#175-the-squeamish-ossifrage-a-129-digit-number-falls).

## S

**Scherbius, Arthur**
German engineer (1878 to May 1929) who filed the patent for a rotor cipher machine on 23 February 1918 and sold it as Enigma from 1923. His company went on under other owners with the German military as its main customer.
See [Chapter 12](12-Rotors-and-Enigma.md#123-enigma-from-a-postal-congress-to-the-wehrmacht).

**Schmidt, Hans-Thilo**
A clerk of the Reichswehr cipher office (1888 to 1943) who sold Enigma papers to France from 1931, for money and from a grudge against his brother. He was arrested in 1943 and died in custody that September.
See [Chapter 12](12-Rotors-and-Enigma.md#127-the-spy-the-french-officer-and-the-papers).

**Sealed packet**
A sealed envelope with a note of a result, deposited with the Paris Academy of Sciences from the 1700s and kept unopened. Wolfgang Doeblin's, sent in February 1940, was opened in May 2000.
See [Chapter 1](01-Why-People-Hid-Writing.md#15-the-anagram-a-secret-that-proves-you-were-first).

**Security key**
A device that holds a private key for each website it is registered with and signs the site's challenge together with the site's real name, so a look-alike page gets a useless answer. The FIDO Alliance published U2F in December 2014; WebAuthn followed in March 2019 and passkeys in 2022.
See [Chapter 19](19-Hardware-Quantum-and-the-Long-View.md#195-keys-you-hold-security-keys-and-passkeys).

**SHA-1**
A 160-bit hash published in April 1995 as FIPS 180-1. Wang Xiaoyun (born 1966, the year usually given), who found the first MD5 collisions in 2004, attacked it in 2005 with Yin and Yu, SHAttered found a collision in 2017, and a chosen-prefix collision followed in 2020.
See [Chapter 18](18-The-Internet-Era.md#189-hash-functions-break).

**SHA-3 (Keccak)**
The hash NIST chose on 2 October 2012 after a five-year contest, built differently from SHA-2 as insurance after the breaks of MD5 and SHA-1. FIPS 202 followed in August 2015.
See [Chapter 18](18-The-Internet-Era.md#189-hash-functions-break).

**Shannon, Claude**
American mathematician (30 April 1916 to 24 February 2001) at Bell Labs from 1941 to 1956, then MIT. His classified report of 1 September 1945 and his paper of October 1949 gave secrecy a mathematical definition.
See [Chapter 15](15-Shannon-and-the-Agencies.md#153-shannon-at-bell-labs-the-report-and-the-paper).

**Shift register**
A row of one-bit memory cells that shifts along on every clock tick, with a new bit computed from the others by XOR. It is cheap and fast and has a long period, but it is linear, so an attacker who knows the design reads the state from a few output bits.
See [Chapter 15](15-Shannon-and-the-Agencies.md#158-from-rotors-to-shift-registers-and-the-people-who-lost-the-keys).

**Shor's algorithm**
Peter Shor's quantum algorithm of 1994 for factoring and discrete logarithms, which would break RSA, Diffie-Hellman and elliptic curves. It has run only on toy numbers, because it needs a machine that does not exist.
See [Chapter 19](19-Hardware-Quantum-and-the-Long-View.md#198-a-computer-made-of-physics-feynman-to-shor-and-grover).

**SIGABA**
The American rotor machine (also called ECM Mark II) that William Friedman and Frank Rowlett designed from 1935 and that was in service from 1941. Control rotors decided which cipher rotors stepped, so the stepping was irregular, and no Axis break of it is known.
See [Chapter 13](13-Bletchley-Park.md#1313-the-machines-that-held).

**Signal protocol**
The Double Ratchet, designed in 2013 by Moxie Marlinspike and Trevor Perrin (first called the Axolotl ratchet), which gives a conversation forward secrecy without both people being online. WhatsApp adopted it in April 2016, and Off-the-Record messaging (2004) is its direct ancestor.
See [Chapter 18](18-The-Internet-Era.md#1813-end-to-end-encryption-from-otr-to-signal).

**SIGSALY**
The first digital voice cipher, first working on 15 July 1943 between the Pentagon and London. A vocoder turned speech into twelve numbers fifty times a second, a key from a pressed record was added modulo 6, and the record was destroyed; it carried about 3,000 conferences and was never known to be broken.
See [Chapter 15](15-Shannon-and-the-Agencies.md#152-sigsaly-the-voice-that-went-digital).

**SIKE**
A post-quantum key exchange from isogenies between elliptic curves with the smallest keys of any candidate. Castryck and Decru broke it in July 2022, after Ward Beullens had broken Rainbow in February.
See [Chapter 19](19-Hardware-Quantum-and-the-Long-View.md#1910-the-nist-competition-2016-to-2025).

**Simonetta, Cicco**
Chief secretary of the Sforza dukes of Milan from 1450 (born about 1410 at Caccuri in Calabria). His thirteen rules, dated Pavia, 4 July 1474, are the oldest known European writing on breaking ciphers.
See [Chapter 6](06-Italy-Ambassadors-and-Nomenclators.md#66-simonettas-rules-1474).

**Skipjack**
The NSA's own block cipher, with a 64-bit block, an 80-bit key and 32 rounds, designed for the Clipper chip of 1993. It was classified until June 1998, and in 1999 Biham, Biryukov and Shamir broke 31 of its 32 rounds.
See [Chapter 16](16-Feistel-Lucifer-and-DES.md#1613-after-des-other-ciphers-of-the-era).

**Skytale**
The staff of Sparta. If it was a cipher, a strip wound on a rod of a given thickness is a transposition; Thomas Kelly argued in 1998 that the early sources show only an official staff, and nobody has found one.
See [Chapter 3](03-Greece-and-Rome.md#36-sparta-and-the-skytale).

**Smart card**
A card with a chip that holds a person's identity and refuses to answer after a few wrong guesses. Roland Moreno (1945 to 2012) filed the patent in 1974, and the idea became the chip in the French bank card, the SIM and the passport.
See [Chapter 19](19-Hardware-Quantum-and-the-Long-View.md#193-keys-in-boxes-and-cards-hsms-and-smart-cards).

**Soro, Giovanni**
Venetian appointed secretary of ciphers to the Council of Ten about 1506. He made Venice's keys and read other states' ciphers, and Kahn calls him the West's first great cryptanalyst.
See [Chapter 6](06-Italy-Ambassadors-and-Nomenclators.md#65-the-cipher-office).

**Spectre and Meltdown**
Flaws in processors, public on 3 January 2018, that come from the processor's guessing ahead to gain speed and had been present for about twenty years. They made cryptographers treat that guessing as attack surface.
See [Chapter 19](19-Hardware-Quantum-and-the-Long-View.md#196-the-leak-in-the-open-timing-power-faults-and-caches).

**SSH**
Tatu Ylönen's program of July 1995 in Helsinki for logging in to a remote computer without sending passwords in the clear. OpenSSH followed in December 1999, and the first key a server shows is trusted on first use.
See [Chapter 18](18-The-Internet-Era.md#184-ssh).

**SSL**
The Secure Sockets Layer, begun at Netscape in 1994 and 1995 from a design by Kipp Hickman, which put the padlock in the first browsers. SSL 3.0 of 1996 came from a team led by Taher Elgamal with Paul Kocher as co-author, it became TLS in 1999, and SSL 2.0 was deprecated in 2011.
See [Chapter 18](18-The-Internet-Era.md#182-ssl-at-netscape).

**Station HYPO**
The Navy's Combat Intelligence Unit in the basement of the Fourteenth Naval District headquarters at Pearl Harbor, under Joseph Rochefort. It read enough of JN-25 in May 1942 to find Midway as the target.
See [Chapter 14](14-The-Rest-of-the-War.md#146-station-hypo-jn-25-and-midway).

**Steganography**
Hiding that a message exists at all, as in a tattooed scalp or a message under wax. The word is Greek for "covered writing" and was made popular by Johannes Trithemius.
See [Chapter 1](01-Why-People-Hid-Writing.md#12-hiding-coding-and-scrambling).

**Superencipherment**
Scrambling the code words or numbers of a code book a second time by a secret rule, such as adding a secret number digit by digit. It became the standard system of armies and ministries for a hundred years, and it is weak if the secret number is reused.
See [Chapter 10](10-The-Telegraph.md#102-codes-by-the-price-of-a-word).

**Sweet32**
The attack of 2016 by Bhargavan and Leurent that exploited the 64-bit block of Triple DES, against HTTPS and OpenVPN connections. By the birthday bound, equal blocks begin to be likely after about 2^32 blocks, which is 32 gigabytes.
See [Chapter 16](16-Feistel-Lucifer-and-DES.md#168-triple-des-and-the-long-retirement).

## T

**T-310**
An East German electronic teleprinter encryptor, in service from the 1970s, which carried the highest-level traffic of the party, the state, the Stasi and the army. It had no rotors, and its algorithm became known to outsiders only after 1990.
See [Chapter 15](15-Shannon-and-the-Agencies.md#157-the-soviet-side-fialka-and-the-east-german-machines).

**Tabula recta**
Trithemius's square table in which each row is Caesar's alphabet shifted one place more than the last, so that the alphabet changes at every letter. As a cipher it has no key, so anyone who knows the method can break it.
See [Chapter 7](07-Printed-Ciphers.md#75-a-table-with-no-key).

**Tally**
In China, a token for secret communication. The eight tallies of the Liu Tao are rods whose lengths are their meanings, and the bronze tiger tallies are tokens split in two, so that a messenger's half proves an order is real.
See [Chapter 8](08-The-World-Beyond-Europe.md#82-china-tallies-split-letters-and-matching-halves).

**TEMPEST**
The US government's code name for stopping cipher equipment from leaking its plain text through electrical signals. It began in 1943 when Bell Labs found that the 131-B2 teleprinter mixer showed plaintext as spikes on an oscilloscope.
See [Chapter 19](19-Hardware-Quantum-and-the-Long-View.md#192-the-first-leak-bell-labs-1943-and-tempest).

**Testery**
The section formed in July 1942 under Major Ralph Tester to read Tunny, staffed by linguists, classicists and a few mathematicians. It found the remaining wheel settings once the Newmanry had the chi settings.
See [Chapter 13](13-Bletchley-Park.md#1310-the-testery-the-newmanry-and-the-wrens-at-the-colossus).

**Tiltman, John**
British Army officer and self-taught codebreaker (25 May 1894 to 10 August 1982) who recovered both texts and four thousand characters of key from the Athens-Vienna depth of 30 August 1941. That key stream went to the Research Section, where Tutte worked.
See [Chapter 13](13-Bletchley-Park.md#139-tunny-a-depth-a-rectangle-and-a-machine-nobody-had-seen).

**Timing attack**
Paul Kocher's attack of 1996 showing that a correct implementation can leak a key through how long it takes. It led to blinding and to constant-time code.
See [Chapter 19](19-Hardware-Quantum-and-the-Long-View.md#196-the-leak-in-the-open-timing-power-faults-and-caches).

**TLS 1.3**
The version of the protocol published on 10 August 2018 as RFC 8446, edited by Eric Rescorla (a revised text, RFC 9846, replaced it in July 2026). It gives every connection forward secrecy and a one-round-trip handshake, and removed every feature earlier attacks had used.
See [Chapter 18](18-The-Internet-Era.md#183-from-ssl-to-tls-13-and-the-attacks-with-names).

**Transposition cipher**
A cipher that rearranges the letters of the message without changing them. The skytale, if it was a cipher, is the oldest example.
See [Chapter 3](03-Greece-and-Rome.md#36-sparta-and-the-skytale).

**Triple DES**
DES applied three times, proposed by Walter Tuchman of IBM in 1979 to keep a DES-compatible cipher with a longer key. NIST disallowed it for encryption after 31 December 2023.
See [Chapter 16](16-Feistel-Lucifer-and-DES.md#168-triple-des-and-the-long-retirement).

**Trithemius, Johannes**
Abbot (1 February 1462 to 13 December 1516), of Sponheim from 1483, who wrote the Steganographia (about 1499), a book of hidden writing dressed as magic whose third book was solved only in 1996 to 1998, and the Polygraphia (finished 1508, printed 1518), the first printed book on cryptography.
See [Chapter 7](07-Printed-Ciphers.md#72-the-abbot-of-sponheim).

**Trusted Platform Module (TPM)**
A small chip on the motherboard, specified by the Trusted Computing Group (formed in 2003), that stores keys and releases one only if the software that loaded matches what it was when the key was stored. Windows used it for BitLocker from 2007 and made TPM 2.0 a requirement for Windows 11 in 2021.
See [Chapter 19](19-Hardware-Quantum-and-the-Long-View.md#194-keys-in-the-computer-tpms-enclaves-and-instructions).

**Tunny**
The British name for the Lorenz cipher and its traffic. It was read from 1942 and routinely from 1944, although no machine was in British hands until the end of the war.
See [Chapter 13](13-Bletchley-Park.md#139-tunny-a-depth-a-rectangle-and-a-machine-nobody-had-seen).

**Turing, Alan**
Mathematician (23 June 1912 to 7 June 1954) who designed the logic of the bombe in 1939, invented Banburismus and headed Hut 8. He was convicted in 1952 and pardoned in 2013.
See [Chapter 13](13-Bletchley-Park.md#135-the-crib-the-menu-and-the-bombe).

**Tutte, Bill**
Chemist-turned-mathematician (14 May 1917 to 2 May 2002) who by January 1942 worked out the wheels and motor of the Lorenz machine from Tiltman's key stream, and then devised the statistical method that Colossus later ran.
See [Chapter 13](13-Bletchley-Park.md#139-tunny-a-depth-a-rectangle-and-a-machine-nobody-had-seen).

**Twig runes**
A way of writing each rune as a pair of numbers, its family and its place in the family, recorded in Scandinavian inscriptions of the 1100s. They show skill in the rune row, probably not a hiding from enemies.
See [Chapter 5](05-The-Medieval-World.md#54-runes-that-hide-inside-runes).

**Two-time pad**
Two messages enciphered with the same pad page. Subtracting one from the other cancels the page, and this is how Venona got in.
See [Chapter 14](14-The-Rest-of-the-War.md#1413-venona-i-the-pad-printed-twice).

**Typex**
The British rotor machine, adopted in 1937, with five rotors of which two did not move, several turnover notches per rotor and a printer. It carried British secrets through the war and has no known wartime break.
See [Chapter 13](13-Bletchley-Park.md#1313-the-machines-that-held).

## U

**UKUSA Agreement**
The treaty of 5 March 1946, building on the BRUSA agreement of May 1943, that tied British and American signals intelligence together. Canada, Australia and New Zealand joined later, and its text was released in 2010.
See [Chapter 15](15-Shannon-and-the-Agencies.md#155-from-the-black-chamber-to-the-nsa-and-gchq).

**Ultra**
The name for the intelligence from Enigma decrypts, held above Top Secret and reported to a short list. It was kept secret by rules, cover stories and the Official Secrets Act until 1974.
See [Chapter 13](13-Bletchley-Park.md#137-ultra-using-a-secret-and-keeping-it).

**Unicity distance**
Shannon's measure of how much ciphertext it takes before only one key fits. It says when a cryptogram has a single answer and nothing about how much work finding the answer takes.
See [Chapter 15](15-Shannon-and-the-Agencies.md#154-what-the-1949-paper-proved).

## V

**Venona**
The project begun on 1 February 1943 at Arlington Hall to read Soviet messages enciphered with pad pages that had been printed twice in 1942. It identified spies, ran until 1980, and its first decrypts were released in 1995.
See [Chapter 14](14-The-Rest-of-the-War.md#1413-venona-i-the-pad-printed-twice).

**Vernam, Gilbert**
AT&T engineer (1890 to 1960) who made the teleprinter cipher of 1917 (US patent 1,310,719, granted 22 July 1919). With a random, non-repeating key it became the one-time pad.
See [Chapter 11](11-Radio-War-and-the-One-Time-Pad.md#117-vernam-mauborgne-and-the-cipher-that-cannot-be-broken).

**Viète, François**
French lawyer and councillor (1540 to 1603) who read Philip II's intercepted letters for Henri IV in 1589 and 1590. He also introduced letters for unknowns and constants in algebra; no account of his method survives.
See [Chapter 9](09-Queens-Spies-and-Black-Chambers.md#96-a-king-who-trusted-his-cipher-spain-and-viete).

**Vigenère, Blaise de**
French diplomat (5 April 1523 to 19 February 1596) who printed the Traicté des chiffres in 1586, with a true autokey. His name is on the repeating-keyword cipher, which he did not invent.
See [Chapter 7](07-Printed-Ciphers.md#79-vigenere-and-a-name-that-stuck-to-the-wrong-cipher).

**Voynich manuscript**
An unread book on vellum from 1404 to 1438, by radiocarbon, with plants, stars and baths in an unknown script. Its length rules out a simple substitution of a known language; it is held at Yale's Beinecke Library as MS 408.
See [Chapter 5](05-The-Medieval-World.md#58-the-book-nobody-can-read).

## W

**Walsingham, Francis**
Elizabeth's principal secretary from 1573 (about 1532 to 1590), a hard-working Protestant. His man was Mary's courier, which is how her letters were read.
See [Chapter 9](09-Queens-Spies-and-Black-Chambers.md#92-mary-queen-of-scots-and-the-barrel-at-chartley).

**Welchman, Gordon**
Cambridge mathematician (15 June 1906 to 8 October 1985) who ran Hut 6, invented the diagonal board and turned Enigma breaking into an organized flow of work. His book The Hut Six Story appeared in 1982.
See [Chapter 13](13-Bletchley-Park.md#135-the-crib-the-menu-and-the-bombe).

**Wheel cipher**
A stack of disks on an axle, each with its own scrambled alphabet around the edge, so that the order of the disks is the key. Jefferson described one with 36 disks in the 1790s, and Bazeries reinvented it in 1891 with 20; de Viaris broke Bazeries's in 1893 with a guessed word.
See [Chapter 10](10-The-Telegraph.md#1011-the-wheel-cipher-jefferson-bazeries-and-de-viaris).

## Y

**Yardley, Herbert O.**
Head of the US Army's first cipher section (13 April 1889 to 7 August 1958) and of the Cipher Bureau in New York. His book of 1931 was the first full account of a codebreaking office by someone who ran it.
See [Chapter 11](11-Radio-War-and-the-One-Time-Pad.md#118-yardley-and-the-american-black-chamber).

## Z

**Zimmermann telegram**
The message of 16 January 1917, in code 0075, offering Mexico an alliance against the United States. Room 40 read it, the British showed the Americans a second copy taken in Mexico to hide the first, and the United States declared war on 6 April 1917.
See [Chapter 11](11-Radio-War-and-the-One-Time-Pad.md#113-the-zimmermann-telegram).

**Zygalski, Henryk**
Polish mathematician (1908 to 1978) who devised the perforated sheets of 1938. He reached Britain in 1943 and taught mathematics after the war.
See [Chapter 12](12-Rotors-and-Enigma.md#1211-what-became-of-them).

**Zygalski's sheets**
Perforated cards, a 26-by-26 grid for each rotor order and each position of the left-hand rotor, with a hole wherever a female could occur. Stacked and shifted by the ground setting of each message, they let light through only at settings that fit every female.
See [Chapter 12](12-Rotors-and-Enigma.md#129-the-race-grill-cyclometer-catalog-sheets-and-bomba).
